**Top three key risks**

The three risks below are ranked by how directly they could hit reported earnings and the balance sheet. The first is credit risk in the loan book. The second is concentration in the credit card business together with regulatory caps on its pricing. The third is dependence on information technology systems and third-party providers.

**1. Credit quality of the loan portfolio and adequacy of the expected credit loss (ECL) allowance**

The company states that it expects reported non-performing loans to rise in absolute terms as the loan portfolio grows, and that the portfolio could perform worse than anticipated. Its credit scoring models are described as subject to error, and the company says that limited and unreliable borrower information in its markets means it "cannot guarantee that our current or future reserves for credit losses will be sufficient to cover actual losses." If losses exceed estimates, the company may have to raise provisions, which would reduce earnings. The filing ties this exposure to borrower financial condition, macroeconomic expectations, interest rates and the legal environment. Two further exposures compound it. Financial instruments, cash and deposits at central banks made up 54.7% of total assets at December 31, 2025, against 55.0% a year earlier, so investment and derivative losses could also affect income. The company also says its hedging strategy may not prevent losses, and it has chosen not to hedge foreign exchange exposure arising from its investments in Brazil, Colombia and Mexico.

**2. Concentration in credit card revenue and regulatory limits on card fees and interest**

Credit and prepaid card fees and interest on credit card balances remain the core revenue streams. In 2025 they represented 10.9% and 29.1% of revenue respectively, a combined 40.0% (illustrative: the sum of the two disclosed shares). In 2024 the same two shares were 12.0% and 33.0%, a combined 45.0%. Concentration is falling, but the filing describes the revenue mix as still heavily weighted toward cards. The company names two regulatory developments as threats. The first is the Over Indebtedness Law of July 2, 2021, which requires specific credit disclosures, and Decree No. 11,567, which set a "base minimum" (mínimo existencial) of R$600.0 for over-indebtedness negotiations. The company says these may push customers away from its credit portfolio. The second is the possibility that fees or interest rates become capped. The filing states that revenue would be harmed if such caps were introduced or, where they already exist, reduced. The company also notes that open finance and recent rule changes have made it easier for customers to move credit obligations to competitors, which would reduce its interest income.

**3. Dependence on information technology systems, data centers and third-party providers**

All customer transactions run through the mobile application, so an application failure makes the platform unavailable to customers. The company states that it "do[es] not operate all of our systems on a real-time basis" and that it has "experienced, and may in the future experience, disruptions, outages and other performance problems." It also says it does not maintain insurance policies specifically for property and business interruption, so losses from such events would be borne directly. Mastercard authorizes the company's credit and prepaid card transactions, and some providers are described as "the sole source or one of a limited number of sources." The company says it cannot easily move its operations to another cloud provider on similar terms. Revenue growth itself adds strain: the company reports that revenue rose 37%, from US$11,517.1 million in 2024 to US$15,774.7 million in 2025, while full-time employees rose 15%, from 8,716 to 10,027, and it notes that growth "has placed and will continue to place significant demands" on its systems.

**Industry vertical risk**

The filing does not compare the company's risk with other industry verticals, and it provides no sector-level data, so no ranking of this kind can be drawn from the resource. What the filing does establish is the risk profile within its own business. The company operates as a digital financial services platform and payment institution in Brazil, Mexico and Colombia, and it describes intense competition from traditional banks, other neobanks, payment providers and new fintech entrants. It also describes regulation that is changing quickly. Recent examples include the Central Bank of Brazil's crypto framework (BCB Resolutions No. 519, 520 and 521, effective February 2, 2026, with a 270-day transition period), the AI Bill of Law No. 2,338/23 still under discussion in the Brazilian House of Representatives, and Brazil's presidential elections in October 2026. Taken together, these points put the company in a regulation-heavy and competition-heavy position. The resource does not allow that position to be measured against peers.

**Cyclicality**

The filing says the electronic payments industry "depends heavily on the overall level of consumer spending." It links spending to consumer confidence, discretionary income and purchasing habits, and it warns that a sustained deterioration in economic conditions, including higher unemployment or higher interest rates, would reduce the number and size of card transactions. Because the company earns interest on card balances, a downturn would affect the credit side as well, through higher non-performing loans and larger provisions. Seasonality adds a second, shorter cycle. Holiday months such as Black Friday and Christmas lift transaction volume, while February produces lower revenue because it has fewer calendar days. The filing does not quantify how sensitive revenue or provisions are to a specific change in GDP, unemployment or interest rates, so the cyclical impact can be described but not measured from this resource.

**Risk quantification**

The most measurable exposures are the following. Card-related revenue (fees plus interest) fell from 45.0% of revenue in 2024 to 40.0% in 2025, which is a shift in concentration rather than a decline in absolute terms, since total revenue grew 37%. Financial and cash assets were 54.7% of total assets in 2025, a small fall from 55.0%, and they carry gain-and-loss volatility the filing describes as potentially "significant." The insider ownership figures show that David Vélez holds 88.3% of the Class B shares, which carry approximately 74.4% of the voting power. That concentration does not change the three operating risks above, but it limits outside shareholders' influence over how management responds to them. The company reports that it has been profitable since fiscal year 2023. The resource gives no figures for non-performing loans, ECL allowance levels or provision amounts, so credit risk cannot be quantified further from this document.

**Downside protections**

The filing offers several partial protections, though none is complete. Revenue is spread across fees, interest, investments, insurance and payments, and the company has regional operations in Mexico and Colombia. It states that it has "several controls in place" over provision adequacy, applies human oversight to generative AI, and maintains disaster recovery and business continuity arrangements at its data centers. Its multi-source vendor approach is noted as a means of reducing reliance on a single provider, although Mastercard is described as a sole source. Most customer arrangements are not long-term contracts, however. The filing states that customers "have no obligation to continue to use our products" and that deposits "may be withdrawn without notice." That means the company's revenue visibility is limited, and the business relies on continuous customer acquisition rather than contracted recurring income.

**What would prove this assessment wrong**

The assessment would be wrong if the company reports stable non-performing loans and a stable or falling ECL allowance through an economic slowdown, which would indicate that credit scoring is working better than the filing suggests. It would also be wrong if card fees and card interest hold up after the R$600.0 base minimum and any new Brazilian fee caps take effect, which would show the revenue base is more durable than the regulatory discussion implies. On technology risk, the view would be wrong if the company reports no material outages in peak periods over several reporting periods while growing its customer base. Finally, if the Central Bank of Brazil's crypto framework and the open finance rules proceed without material changes to the company's operating model, the regulatory risk described here would be lower than the filing's language suggests. The resource itself is incomplete: the risk factor text ends during the discussion of hedging, so any further risk disclosures in the remainder of the 20-F are not reflected in this analysis.
