Alphabet's three most consequential risks, as the 10-K describes them, are (1) dependence on advertising, which produced more than 70% of 2025 revenues, in a market that the company says AI is reshaping; (2) regulatory and antitrust exposure, including remedies that could restrict how Search and advertising technology operate; and (3) the capital and supply commitments required to build AI and cloud infrastructure. **Risk 1: Advertising concentration and AI-driven change in how ads are delivered**

Industry vertical risk. The filing does not benchmark advertising against other industries, so the comparison here is qualitative. The company's exposure is narrow in a specific sense: more than 70% of 2025 revenues came from online advertising, and the company's own description of its partners is that advertisers, distribution partners, publishers, and content providers "can terminate their contracts with us at any time." That means the revenue base is not protected by long-dated contracts. The filing also identifies threats that are specific to digital advertising: technologies that block ads or make personalization harder, and integrations by online service providers that could impair third-party advertising. Against this, the company's broader business description (Cloud, devices, subscriptions, Other Bets) suggests some diversification, but the filing does not quantify any of those lines in the material provided.

Cyclicality. The filing states that advertiser spending "tend[s] to correlate with overall economic conditions" and that adverse macroeconomic conditions "have affected, and may in the future affect" demand. It also notes cyclical nature and seasonality as drivers of period-to-period fluctuation. Because advertising is most of the revenue base, a downturn in advertiser budgets passes through to total revenue more directly than it would for a company with a larger non-advertising base.

Risk quantification. Illustratively, if advertising is 70% of revenue and all other revenue were unchanged, a 10% decline in advertising would reduce total revenue by about 7% (0.70 × 10%). This is a back-of-the-envelope figure, not a company estimate, and the actual sensitivity depends on how the other 30% behaves. Further risk markers in the text: the company says its revenue growth rate "could decline over time," listing pricing decreases, shifts to lower-priced products, and changes in how users access information. The filing states that the company "may not adapt effectively" to AI-driven ad formats and that competitors may offer experiences "substantially similar to or better than ours."

Downside protections. The filing offers few contractual protections for advertising revenue, because the partner relationships are terminable at will. The mitigants it does describe are diversification across more than one product line and geographic mix (international revenue was approximately 52% of 2025 consolidated revenues), and the company's stated intent to keep investing in ad formats. These reduce concentration in one country but not in one revenue type.

**Risk 2: Regulatory and antitrust exposure**

Industry vertical risk. The filing presents this as the risk with the most directly quantifiable legal consequences. It describes multiple large antitrust matters in the US and abroad, a dense set of digital regulations (the EU Digital Markets Act, the EU Digital Services Act, the EU AI Act, and state-level US laws), and a growing number of jurisdictions adopting rules on privacy, content moderation, copyright, and digital taxes. Compared with the advertising demand risk in Risk 1, which depends on advertiser behavior, this risk is set by government action, and the filing says outcomes are uncertain and that legal liability estimates are "speculative."

Cyclicality. Regulatory risk is not cyclical in the economic sense, but it is episodic and sequential. Several matters are at distinct stages. In the Search case, a final judgment was entered in December 2025 imposing restrictions on distribution and requiring data sharing and syndication to certain competitors; the company appealed in January 2026 and sought to pause certain remedies, and the DOJ and states appealed in February 2026. In the advertising technology case, the April 2025 decision found the publisher tools unfairly excluded rivals while the advertiser tools and the DoubleClick and AdMeld acquisitions were not found anticompetitive; remedies were argued in September and November 2025, and the filing notes that the DOJ's proposal "includes structural remedies that could harm our business." A separate Texas-led state trial will follow the advertising technology remedies decision.

Risk quantification. The text supports several specific data points. The state legislatures considered more than 1,000 AI-related bills in 2025. California and New York passed frontier AI laws in 2025. More than 140 countries are negotiating changes to international tax policy, including a 15% global minimum tax, and the US imposed a 1% excise tax on share repurchases. The EU AI Act became effective on August 1, 2024, with full applicability after a two-year transition. The filing does not give dollar amounts for any fine, judgment, or accrual, so the financial scale cannot be measured from the material provided.

Downside protections. The filing identifies statutory safe harbors, including Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act and the Digital Millennium Copyright Act, that limit liability for linking, caching, ranking, and hosting. These protections are real but the filing says they are under legislative and litigation pressure in the US and EU. The company also states that it is "cooperating with these investigations" and appealing where appropriate. Neither is a financial cushion.

**Risk 3: Capital intensity and supply of AI infrastructure**

Industry vertical risk. The filing describes the company's AI and cloud build-out as capital-heavy and supply-constrained in a way that its advertising business is not. It identifies several limits: power, water, and land availability; energy supply "constrained globally"; AI chips "limited to a small number of qualified suppliers"; and long-lead-time energy arrangements. It also states that certain devices and infrastructure components are available from only one or limited sources. Cloud and devices are described as lower-margin than the core business, and the filing says margins on devices "have had, and may continue to have, an adverse effect on our consolidated margins." Peer comparison data is not included, so the statement is about the company's own mix, not about the industry.

Cyclicality. The filing ties this risk to the cycle of AI demand and to industry-wide capacity. It warns that if it has "excess capacity that we cannot easily redeploy" during "an industry downturn," it may incur liabilities and fail to receive payments from counterparties. The cyclical exposure therefore runs in the opposite direction from advertising: the commitments are fixed in advance, while demand for the capacity may not be.

Risk quantification. The text lists these concrete exposures: significant leasing arrangements with third-party operators; "a number of large, long-duration commercial agreements" that increase liabilities if the company or counterparties fail to perform; "significant and increasing" costs and "new liabilities, including contingent liabilities"; and changes to the useful lives of property and equipment, which the filing says could change "the period over which we expect to benefit from the asset." The filing also says the company "expect[s] to significantly expand our investment in property and equipment." Dollar amounts for commitments, depreciation, or capital expenditures are not in the provided material, so the scale cannot be sized.

Downside protections. The filing describes a few partial offsets: the company can redeploy some capacity internally, it negotiates volume-based supply terms, and it has a custom-built TPU line that it describes as a way to reduce dependence on third-party accelerators. The filing does not state how much of the capacity is contracted to customers, so the extent of protection cannot be assessed.

**Overall assessment**

The risks interact. The advertising business is the source of most current revenue and is exposed to both AI-driven shifts in ad delivery and to economic cycles. The regulatory actions focus on the same advertising and search business, and the remedies under consideration (restrictions on distribution, data sharing, syndication, and in the ad technology case, possible structural remedies) could reduce the profitability of the core business that funds the AI build-out. The infrastructure commitments, in turn, are long-dated and largely fixed, while the revenue they are meant to support depends on AI products and cloud demand that the filing describes as highly competitive and unproven in its economics. The filing says that some new investments "may not be commercially viable," which means the company is taking on fixed obligations on the expectation that new revenue will arrive.

The strongest argument against this view is that the company has diversified geographically, has a larger product portfolio than advertising alone, and has safe harbors and appeal rights that may limit outcomes. The filing supports that partly. It does not, however, provide the segment or cash-flow data needed to test how much of the non-advertising business offsets an advertising decline, or how large the infrastructure commitments are against cash generation. Those are the figures that would confirm or weaken this assessment.

Several observations would prove this view wrong. Advertising revenue growth that holds up through an economic slowdown and through an AI-driven shift in ad formats would indicate the concentration risk is lower than described. A final ad technology remedy that does not require structural divestiture, and an appellate result on Search that leaves distribution unrestricted, would reduce the regulatory risk materially. Disclosure showing that the AI infrastructure commitments are largely matched by contracted customer revenue, rather than speculative capacity, would reduce the supply and idle-capacity risk. Conversely, evidence that the company is extending asset useful lives or taking on further long-duration commitments while advertising growth slows would strengthen the case that these risks are rising together.

## Year-over-Year Trends for Alphabet

Revenue grew from 282,836 in 2022 to 307,394 in 2023, 350,018 in 2024 and 402,836 in 2025. Growth was 8.7% in 2023, 13.9% in 2024 and 15.1% in 2025. The slowdown in 2023 was the weakest year in the series, and the two later years show acceleration rather than stability.

Gross margin rose each year, from 55.4% to 56.6%, 58.2% and 59.7%. The increase is steady, at roughly 1 to 2 points a year, and indicates that cost of revenue grew more slowly than revenue.

EBITDA margin rose from 30.1% in 2022 to 31.9% in 2023, then jumped to 38.7% in 2024 and reached 44.9% in 2025. Absolute EBITDA rose from 85,160 to 180,698, roughly 2.1 times the 2022 level. ## Metric-Specific Insights

### EBITDA Margin

In the most recent year, Alphabet's EBITDA margin of 44.9% ranks third of the four companies, behind Microsoft (55.2% in 2025; 62.5% in the 2026 label) and Meta (52.6%), and well ahead of Amazon (23.1%). The improvement is the main positive in the data: Alphabet added 14.8 points between 2022 and 2025, and the 2024 and 2025 increases were the largest of the period. Meta's margin rose more over the same span (32.3% to 52.6%, a 20.3-point gain) but has been flat since 2024, while Alphabet's margin is still rising. The shortfall is that Alphabet remains below Meta and Microsoft, and the gap to Meta has been narrowing since 2024.

### EV/EBITDA

Alphabet's multiple rose from 13.48 to 21.15, a 57% increase over three years, while EBITDA rose 112%. Put another way, if enterprise value is approximated as the multiple times EBITDA, Alphabet's implied enterprise value rose by roughly 3.3 times over the period (illustrative calculation: 13.48 times 85,160 compared with 21.15 times 180,698, in the table's units). That is faster than EBITDA, so part of the increase reflects a higher multiple, not only higher earnings. Relative to its peers, Alphabet was at a discount to Microsoft in every year it can be compared and at a premium to Meta in 2023, 2024 and 2025 (with 2024 nearly equal at 17.17 and 17.14). Alphabet traded above Amazon in 2023 and 2025 and below Amazon in 2022 and 2024 (2024: 17.17 against 19.19). On this single ratio, Alphabet appears fully valued against Meta and Amazon in 2025 and below Microsoft's 2025 multiple, but the table does not include growth-adjusted or forward estimates, so it cannot say whether the premium is warranted.

### FCF Conversion

Alphabet's cash conversion weakened over the period while its peers' did not follow the same path. Meta's ratio was 0.76 in 2025 after a peak of 1.13 in 2023, and Microsoft's was 0.70 in 2025, which is down from 0.84 in 2024 but still above Alphabet's. Alphabet's 2025 ratio of 0.55 is the lowest among the three companies with positive figures apart from Amazon. Alphabet's free cash flow in absolute terms was 73,266 in 2025, close to Microsoft's 71,611 in the same label year and well above Meta's 46,109, but Alphabet's EBITDA is larger, so each unit of EBITDA produced less free cash flow. The resources do not explain the cause, so this is a question the 10-K detail would need to answer.

### Gross Margin

Alphabet's gross margin rose by 4.3 points over three years, from 55.4% to 59.7%, and the increase came in every year. The gap to Meta has remained around 22 to 23 points, which reflects a structurally higher margin at Meta rather than a deterioration at Alphabet. Microsoft's gross margin has been slipping slightly (69.8% in 2024 to 68.8% in 2025 to 67.9% in the 2026 label), so it is no longer expanding while Alphabet is. Amazon's margin rose from 43.8% to 50.3%, which is the same direction as Alphabet's, but from a lower base. Alphabet's cost efficiency, measured this way, is improving in absolute terms and against Amazon, but it has not closed the gap to Meta or Microsoft.

### ROIC

Alphabet's ROIC was 28.7% in 2025 after 29.9% in 2024, and it has been in the high 20s for two years. That level is at or above Microsoft (26.8% in 2025) and well above Meta (22.2%) and Amazon (16.8%). The trend is stable to slightly down in the last year and the level is high, which suggests Alphabet is generating strong returns on the capital it invests. Two points need watching. ROIC edged down in 2025 while EBITDA rose 33%, which suggests that the capital base grew at a similar or faster pace. The resources do not show invested capital, so the cause of the 2025 dip cannot be identified. Also, ROIC uses a different base from EBITDA, so a rising EBITDA margin does not automatically produce a rising ROIC.

### Revenue and Revenue Growth

Alphabet's revenue grew from 282,836 in 2022 to 402,836 in 2025, an increase of 42%. The growth rate was lowest in 2023 (8.7%), then accelerated to 13.9% in 2024 and 15.1% in 2025. This is the most favourable trend in the revenue data, and it means Alphabet is growing faster than Amazon in the last two years. Against Meta, Alphabet is clearly slower: Meta's 22.2% growth in 2025 was roughly 7 points higher. Against Microsoft, the 2026 label shows 17.8%, which is above Alphabet's latest figure but is a different year. The 2023 dip is a fact in the table, but the resources do not explain its cause, so this analysis cannot say whether it was temporary.

## Conclusion

Alphabet's financial health, as shown in these metrics, is strong and improving on profitability, growth and returns. Revenue growth has accelerated for two years, gross margin and EBITDA margin have risen in every year, and ROIC has stayed near 28% to 30%. The weaker signals are in cash generation and valuation. FCF Conversion fell from 1.0 to 0.55, and free cash flow grew far more slowly than EBITDA. The EV/EBITDA multiple rose from 13.48 to 21.15, so the market is paying more per unit of earnings than it did in 2022.

The fundamentals support part of the current valuation. An EBITDA more than doubling, a margin up about 15 points, and ROIC near 29% justify a higher multiple than in 2022. They do not, on their own, explain the whole move in enterprise value, which in the illustrative calculation grew about 3.3 times against 2.1 times for EBITDA. The premium to Meta and Amazon in 2025 depends on the market expecting the growth and margin trends to continue, and the table does not include forward estimates to test that expectation.

The view in this analysis would be proven wrong by any of the following in future data: revenue growth falling back toward the 8.7% seen in 2023, EBITDA margin reversing from 44.9%, ROIC falling below the mid-20s, or FCF Conversion continuing below 0.55. A fall in the multiple toward Meta's 16.25 or Amazon's 15.38 without a corresponding drop in EBITDA would suggest that the premium rested on expectations that the reported figures do not yet confirm.
