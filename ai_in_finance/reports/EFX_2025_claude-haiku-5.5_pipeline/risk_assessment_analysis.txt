**Summary of the three key risks**

Equifax's 10-K names several risks, but three stand out for the company's business and the data in the filing. The first is cybersecurity and data security, where the company has already suffered a material 2017 breach whose costs and effects remain visible in the filing. The second is regulatory and legal exposure, driven by the Consumer Settlement, CFPB supervision, FCRA litigation and new artificial intelligence and privacy rules. The third is economic sensitivity, because demand for the company's credit-related products moves with interest rates, consumer credit and mortgage activity, and management expects U.S. mortgage activity in 2026 to be below 2025. The resources contain no financial statements, segment revenue or margin tables, so the quantification below relies on the figures the risk factors disclose. Where the filing gives no number, this analysis says so rather than estimating one.

**1. Cybersecurity and data security breaches**

*Industry vertical risk.* The filing describes the company as a global data, analytics and technology company that stores personal information on consumers, employees and strategic partners. That profile places it in a high-exposure position compared with a business that holds little sensitive personal data, because each breach can trigger notification duties across multiple U.S. state regimes, which the filing says are disparate, and foreign regimes such as the GDPR. The filing does not provide peer data, so the comparison with other industries rests on this description of the company's own data holdings rather than on a benchmark. The filing also notes that the company is "routinely the target" of attacks from well-resourced actors, including state-sponsored efforts, and that artificial intelligence can make phishing and deepfake attacks more effective. Those points raise the bar for any company in this position.

*Cyclicality.* Cyberattacks are not cyclical in the economic sense. Their consequences, however, interact with the credit cycle. The filing states that the company's reputation and customer relationships "were damaged following a prior material cybersecurity incident, resulting in a negative impact on our revenue for a period of time." A breach therefore hits revenue directly and at the same time as the customer demand that already moves with the economy, so a weak economy and a security incident can compound each other.

*Risk quantification.* The filing gives several concrete exposures:
- The 2017 breach led to the July 2019 Consumer Settlement covering the U.S. Consumer MDL, the FTC, the CFPB, the Attorneys General of 48 states, the District of Columbia and Puerto Rico, and the NYDFS. The settlement became effective on January 11, 2022, and it imposes business practice commitments, including third-party assessments of the information security program.
- The company lost certain key certifications after the 2017 incident. Customers and business partners stopped or paused business with it, and the company "had to spend significant resources on remediation activities" to recover them. The filing does not quantify those costs.
- The company states that it "cannot ensure that our insurance policies in the future will be adequate." Cybersecurity insurance exists, but its limits are not disclosed.
- The company's cloud migration moved most data to third-party hosted systems, which the filing says "may expose us to additional cyber threats."

The risk is not hypothetical for this company, and the filing treats it as a recurring one. A future breach would raise the cost of remediation, could cost certifications again, and would reopen the regulatory exposure described under risk 2.

*Downside protections.* The filing cites "substantial investment in physical and technological security measures, employee training and contractual precautions" and says the company expects to "expend significant additional resources to bolster these protections." It also says these measures "cannot provide absolute security." The company has insurance, but it says that coverage may not be adequate. Protection here depends mostly on operational execution, which the company cannot fully guarantee.

*What would prove this view wrong.* A multi-year period without a material incident, together with certifications that customers renew without interruption and insurance that the company can show covers a plausible loss, would indicate that the protections are working. The filing does not offer evidence of that yet.

**2. Regulatory and legal exposure**

*Industry vertical risk.* The filing describes a regulatory environment that "has tended to become more stringent over time" and that covers consumer financial protection, data protection, privacy, artificial intelligence and cybersecurity. The company is also a consumer reporting agency, which subjects it to the FCRA. The filing adds that the FCRA's attorney fee shifting provision has increased consumer lawsuits "substantially over the past several years." Compared with businesses that face mainly commercial contract disputes, this combination of direct supervision by the CFPB and statutory fee shifting is a heavier and less predictable burden. The filing does not provide an industry benchmark.

*Cyclicality.* Regulatory exposure is largely independent of the economic cycle. It can, however, become more costly when the cycle turns, because the filing says lenders' willingness to extend credit weakens in a weak economy, and regulatory scrutiny of consumer credit practices may rise at such times. The filing does not say this directly, so the link should be treated as an inference.

*Risk quantification.* The following figures are disclosed:
- The CFPB can seek civil money penalties "of up to $1.0 million per day for known violations." The filing states that CFPB actions "have in the past resulted, in monetary penalties and requirements to alter or cease offering affected products and services."
- Under the GDPR, fines can reach "up to 4% of annual worldwide turnover." The filing does not state the company's turnover, so the dollar amount cannot be computed.
- The Consumer Settlement's business practice commitments are described as "extensive." A failure to comply could lead to "enforcement action or contempt proceeding" with fines, penalties and new commitments, which "depending on the amount and type, could have a material adverse effect on our financial condition."
- The company states that it is "regularly involved" in claims, suits and government investigations, and that outcomes "cannot be predicted with certainty."
- New AI rules are multiplying. The filing names EU comprehensive oversight laws, several U.S. state laws and pending federal proposals, and states that the company's "use of artificial intelligence in credit decisioning could lead to enhanced scrutiny."

The filing does not give a total litigation reserve or annual compliance spend, so the size of this exposure can only be described qualitatively.

*Downside protections.* The filing says the company devotes "substantial compliance, legal and operational business resources" to compliance and has "committed resources to enhancing our compliance programs." The Consumer Settlement is also a form of resolution, since many of the disputes it covers were closed under the agreements. These are real mitigants, but the filing also says that the Consumer Settlement's obligations are "extensive" and that compliance requires "a significant amount of attention from management." The settlement therefore reduces one type of uncertainty while adding ongoing obligations.

*What would prove this view wrong.* Evidence would include CFPB and state examinations closing without new enforcement actions, a stable or declining count of FCRA suits, and AI or privacy rules that the company can meet without changing core products. The filing provides no such evidence in the present document.

**3. Economic sensitivity and demand**

*Industry vertical risk.* The filing states that the company's customers and revenues "are sensitive to negative changes in general economic conditions," including interest rates, inflation, unemployment, consumer confidence and home prices. Competitive pressure adds to the picture. The filing says some competitors "have significantly greater financial, technical, marketing or other resources," that some "sell products that compete with ours at lower prices," and that "certain of our key products have experienced declines in per unit pricing." Free or inexpensive consumer information is also expanding, including from government sources and companies offering direct-to-consumer credit services. The filing provides no peer benchmark for how these pressures compare with other industries.

*Cyclicality.* The filing describes the business as cyclical in its demand. It says "demand for our services tends to be correlated to general levels of economic activity and to consumer credit activity." It also says that "our customer base generally suffers when financial markets experience volatility, illiquidity and disruption." The filing notes that the company partly offsets this exposure. Its unemployment claims management business in the Workforce Solutions segment "is countercyclical and may experience negative impacts on revenue and operating profit during periods of improving economic conditions or lower unemployment." The two effects therefore pull in opposite directions, and the net result depends on the mix of economic conditions.

*Risk quantification.* The filing supplies these points:
- Management's 2026 planning assumes U.S. GDP growth "consistent with 2025" and slower international growth than in 2025.
- Management "expect[s] U.S. mortgage credit activity in 2026 to be below the levels of activity seen in 2025." A decline in originations "could have a corresponding negative impact on revenue and operating profit," mainly in Workforce Solutions and USIS.
- Sales outside the U.S. were 23% of total revenue in 2025. International exposure adds currency and macroeconomic sensitivity, and the filing states that the company "generally do[es] not mitigate the risks associated with fluctuating exchange rates."
- The company has a $1.5 billion revolving credit facility and a commercial paper program. A downgrade "would increase the cost of borrowings" and "could limit or, in the case of a significant downgrade, preclude our ability to issue commercial paper." The filing ties ratings partly to "the sensitivity of a company's cash flows to changes in the economy."

The filing does not disclose the size of the mortgage-linked revenue, so the earnings impact of the expected decline cannot be estimated from the document. As an illustration only, if mortgage-linked revenue were a given share of the total, the impact would scale with that share, but the share is not in the resources.

*Downside protections.* Diversification is the main protection. The company operates several segments, including Workforce Solutions and USIS, and the countercyclical unemployment claims business offsets some of the credit-cycle exposure. Government contracts are another source of revenue, though the filing notes that they depend on budget processes and that "the U.S. federal government has taken steps to reduce spending on vendor contracts." Long-term contracts provide less protection than they might, since the filing says "many of our material customer agreements can be terminated by the customer for convenience on limited advance written notice." Data-licensing arrangements also carry fixed terms subject to renewal.

*What would prove this view wrong.* If mortgage originations decline less than management expects, or if the countercyclical business and non-mortgage products grow enough to hold revenue steady through a weak mortgage year, the cyclical concern would be smaller than the filing's framing suggests. Conversely, a period of falling unemployment combined with weaker lending would test whether the countercyclical offset is large enough to matter.

**Overall assessment**

The three risks are linked. A security breach would damage the customer and certification relationships on which revenue depends, and a weak credit cycle would reduce the revenue base that funds the remediation and compliance spending the other two risks require. The filing is most concrete about cybersecurity, because the 2017 incident produced a documented chain of consequences: a settlement, lost certifications, lost revenue for a period and continuing obligations. Regulatory exposure is the hardest to size, since the filing discloses penalty ceilings and the existence of ongoing obligations but no reserves or spending totals. Economic sensitivity is the most quantifiable in direction, since the filing states management's own expectation for 2026 mortgage activity, but it provides no dollar figure for the resulting revenue effect.

The filing is also candid that some protections are weaker than they appear. Many customer agreements can be terminated for convenience on short notice, and the company's insurance may not cover future losses. A reader who wants to size these risks more precisely would need segment revenue and margin data, litigation reserve disclosures, cybersecurity insurance limits and the current status of the Consumer Settlement obligations. None of those appear in the resources provided, so this assessment describes the risk landscape qualitatively wherever the filing is silent on the numbers.
