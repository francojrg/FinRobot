# Mastercard: Top Three Risks From the 2025 Form 10-K Risk Factors

The three risks that the filing's risk factors describe as most likely to affect results are regulatory pressure on the four-party model and network fees, competitive and pricing pressure combined with customer concentration, and information security together with privacy and AI regulation. The provided resources contain no industry comparison data, no cyclicality measures, and no interchange, fee, or loss-rate figures, so the analysis below relies on the filing's own descriptions. Where a number is quantified, it is cited. The only company-level quantities in the risk factors are that approximately 71% of 2025 revenue came from outside the U.S., that a significant portion of revenue is concentrated among the five largest customers (without a percentage), and that Mastercard Foundation holds more than 5% of general voting power.

## Risk 1: Regulation of Interchange, Network Fees, Surcharging and Switching

**Industry vertical risk.** The filing describes this as a regulatory risk specific to Mastercard's position rather than a generic sector risk. Regulators in several jurisdictions have designated parts of the business as "systemically important payment systems," as "specified service providers," or as "critical infrastructure," and the filing notes that any new rule "is magnified by the potential it has to be replicated in, or conflict with, other jurisdictions." The filing does not compare this exposure with other industries, so a relative ranking cannot be drawn from the text. What the text does establish is that the risk operates on three layers at once: interchange (which Mastercard does not earn but which shapes merchant and issuer behavior), network fees (which Mastercard does earn and which regulators have begun to examine), and the right to restrict merchant surcharging, which has already been changed in the U.S. and Canada.

**Cyclicality.** The filing does not tie this risk to the economic cycle. Regulatory action is described as driven by policy and litigation rather than by spending conditions. The one cyclical link is indirect: the filing states that if interchange is too high, merchants may route transactions away from the network, and if it is too low, issuers may withdraw benefits, which would reduce volume regardless of the economic environment.

**Risk quantification.** The filing enumerates the specific exposures without assigning amounts:

- Interchange: "we are devoting substantial resources" to defending the right to set interchange rates in regulatory proceedings, litigation and legislation, and regulatory proceedings "in some cases has resulted" in fines and civil damages "the amount of which could be material." No amounts are given.
- Surcharging: U.S. and Canadian merchants may now surcharge credit cards (subject to limits), and the filing warns that broad adoption could reduce transaction volumes. The timing and extent are not quantified.
- Switching restrictions: the filing says governments have implemented or may implement requirements to route domestic payments through domestic providers, and that "regional groups of countries are considering" limits on regional switching. Because Mastercard switches "substantially all cross-border transactions" on its cards, cross-border restrictions would affect a large part of the revenue base, though the filing does not state the share of revenue that would be affected.
- Brazil: the filing says Brazil now requires payment system operators to extend their responsibility for the financial and settlement integrity of payments to merchants. This is a new cost-bearing obligation that is not quantified.

Against this, the one hard number in the regulatory discussion is the 71% of 2025 revenue generated outside the U.S. (illustratively, that leaves roughly 29% from the U.S.). Every market in that 71% is subject to some version of the regulatory risks above, so the share indicates how much of the business faces the sovereignty and switching measures, not how much will be affected.

**Downside protections.** The filing does not present regulation as a protection. Two structural features limit the damage, though neither is described as a mitigant. First, Mastercard does not issue cards, extend credit, or set the interchange rates or merchant discount rates charged to account holders and merchants, so direct consumer-credit regulation falls mainly on issuers and acquirers. Second, the filing states that Mastercard operates a four-party system with a network fee structure, and that the outcome of interchange litigation "could have a more positive or negative impact on us relative to our competitors," which means the company is not assured the same treatment as three-party rivals. The protection is therefore partial and depends on the outcome of individual proceedings.

## Risk 2: Competitive and Pricing Pressure, Disintermediation and Customer Concentration

**Industry vertical risk.** The filing describes the global payments industry as "highly competitive" and lists competitors in general-purpose networks, domestic and local networks, ACH and real-time account-based systems, digital wallets, fintechs, government-backed digital public infrastructure, and digital currencies. It notes that some competitors "operate three-party payments systems with direct connections to both merchants and consumers, potentially providing competitive advantages," and that some "benefit from lower costs" because they rely on technology. Compared with a traditional bank or lender, the filing's description suggests that the risk comes from the number of competing rails rather than from balance-sheet or credit exposure, but the filing does not provide a comparative ranking.

**Cyclicality.** The filing links pricing and customer behavior to economic conditions. It says customers may mitigate their economic exposure by "limiting the issuance of new Mastercard products and requesting greater incentive or greater cost stability," and that consumers and businesses may reduce spending. Cross-border revenue, which the filing says "fluctuates with the levels and destinations of cross-border travel," is the most clearly cyclical revenue stream discussed. Illustratively, a downturn that reduces both customer issuance and cross-border travel would hit volume and pricing at the same time, and the filing indicates that customers could use that moment to press for larger incentives.

**Risk quantification.** The main quantified exposure is customer concentration. The filing states that "a significant portion of our revenue is concentrated among our five largest customers" but does not give the percentage. The filing also notes that "we earn substantial revenue from customers with nearly-exclusive relationships with our competitors," which means part of the largest-customer revenue could move toward competitors. Other pressures are described qualitatively: incentives and pricing discounts that "we may have to increase" to meet customer demand, large merchants that can "negotiate incentives from us," and real-time account-based schemes with "increasingly lower or subsidized P2M pricing." The filing states that customer consolidation "could result in the substantial loss of business," and that one or more customers "could seek to merge with, or acquire, one of our competitors."

**Downside protections.** The filing's own statements limit the protection that contracts offer. It says many customer relationships are not exclusive, that customers "can reassess their future commitments to us subject to the terms of our contracts," and that business agreements "may not ultimately reduce the risk" of customers moving to competitors. Diversification is present but partial: the 71% non-U.S. revenue mix spreads exposure across geographies, yet the five-customer concentration works in the opposite direction. The filing also describes a network with "exclusive or nearly-exclusive" relationships on the issuing side, which provides some stability in those accounts, but states that these relationships "may make it difficult or cost-prohibitive" to grow with other customers. Government regulation is not presented as a protection against competitive pricing pressure; the filing lists regulation as one of the sources of pressure.

## Risk 3: Information Security, Fraud, Account Data Compromise and Data/AI Regulation

**Industry vertical risk.** The filing states that information security risks "have significantly increased" and that because of Mastercard's "position in the payments value chain, we believe that we are likely to continue to be a target." This is a risk shared across financial technology companies, as the filing itself notes ("We, like other financial technology organizations, routinely are subject to cyber-threats"). Mastercard's specific exposure arises from its switching role, which the filing describes as a central point through which "millions of Mastercard, Visa, Discover, American Express and other types of account holders" transact. Its data-regulation exposure also goes beyond the payments sector, because Recorded Future and AI-based products bring in additional regimes.

**Cyclicality.** The filing does not link cyber risk to the economic cycle. It does note that fraud and attacks have grown with the hybrid workforce and with AI, both of which are structural rather than cyclical developments. Cyclicality could enter indirectly, because the filing says the cumulative effect of multiple compromise events "could increase the impact of the fraud," and reduced consumer confidence after a breach could reduce spending.

**Risk quantification.** The filing states that "to date, we have not experienced any material impact relating to cyber-attacks or other information security breaches," which is the company's own historical record. No loss amounts or incident counts are given. The exposures are enumerated: breach notification obligations that may require "parallel notifications to data protection authorities, cyber authorities and/or law-enforcement, often requiring different thresholds, reporting deadlines and formats"; potential damage claims from account data compromises; regulatory penalties; and costs to repair systems. The filing also states that "insurance coverage may not be adequate" and that the company "cannot be sure that our existing insurance coverage will continue to be available on acceptable terms or at all." Two elements raise the risk level above a generic one: the first is the geopolitical exposure created by the listing of Recorded Future as "undesirable" or "unreliable" in some jurisdictions, and the second is the acquisition-related exposure, since the filing notes that "targets that we acquire have had... data practices that do not initially conform" to company standards.

**Downside protections.** The filing describes several controls: "an enterprise resiliency program," security solutions "designed to prevent, detect and respond to fraud and cyber-attacks," and "controls, processes and practices" for systems and data, along with insurance coverage. The filing also notes that "most of the lawsuits resulting from account data breaches do not involve direct claims against us and while we have releases from many issuers and acquirers." However, the filing is explicit that these protections cannot be assumed to be sufficient: "there can be no assurance that such security solutions will perform as expected," and insurance "may not be adequate." The protections reduce exposure but do not remove it.

## Overall Assessment

The three risks share a common structure. Each depends on decisions made by parties other than Mastercard: central banks and legislatures set interchange and switching rules, customers decide where to route volume and how much incentive to demand, and threat actors decide when and how to attack. The filing describes Mastercard as a network that does not issue cards, extend consumer credit, or set most consumer and merchant prices. That structure limits the company's direct credit exposure, but it also means that the company's revenue depends on the continued success of issuers and acquirers whose relationships with it are, in the filing's words, "not exclusive."

The regulatory risk is the least quantified and possibly the most consequential, because the filing names the same mechanisms in several places (interchange, network fees, surcharging, switching, data localization) and states that each could "materially and adversely" affect results. The competitive risk is more concentrated: the five-customer figure is the single largest identifiable point of dependence, and the filing's statement that a loss of business from any one of those customers "could have a material adverse impact" makes the concentration the sharpest quantitative exposure in the risk factors, even though its size is not disclosed. The information security risk is the most operational, and its history (no material impact to date) is favorable but is not presented by the company as a forecast.

Several items would change this assessment. A disclosed share of revenue from the five largest customers, or a decline in that share, would clarify the competitive exposure. Results from the Note 19 proceedings (which the filing cross-references but which was not provided here) would show whether interchange and litigation outcomes have produced fines or damages. ## Conclusion

Mastercard's fiscal 2022 to 2025 record shows a company growing revenue at high rates, expanding its margins, lifting ROIC every year and converting more than 100% of EBITDA into free cash flow in three of four years. Its weak points are a gross margin and EBITDA margin that remain below Visa, a fiscal 2023 cash conversion below 1.0, and a dependence on a single strong year (fiscal 2025) to show the margin gains. The resource does not identify the causes of any of these movements, so the 10-K's management discussion should be checked before attributing them.

The valuation is the harder part. At 25.38 times EBITDA, Mastercard trades at the same multiple as Visa, whose margins are higher but whose growth in fiscal 2025 was lower. On the figures in the resource, the fiscal 2025 results justify a multiple in this range: ROIC above 50% and rising revenue growth of 16.4% support a premium. They do not clearly justify the 29.22 multiple of fiscal 2024, which was set before the fiscal 2025 earnings were reported. The current multiple is therefore supported by the latest year's results but would be exposed if the growth and margin improvements did not persist.

The view would be wrong if any of the following occurred: Mastercard's revenue growth falls back toward the 9% to 11% range that American Express and Visa reported in fiscal 2024 and 2025; its EBITDA margin fails to stay above 60%; or its ROIC declines from the 58.2% level in fiscal 2025. Any of these would weaken the case for a multiple at parity with Visa.
