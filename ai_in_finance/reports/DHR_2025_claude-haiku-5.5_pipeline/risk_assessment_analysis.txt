# Danaher Corporation: Top Three Risks from the FY2025 Item 1A Disclosure

The three risks that the filing's own language and figures point to most strongly are, in order: (1) pricing and reimbursement pressure from healthcare cost-containment policy, (2) concentrated exposure to non-U.S. markets, especially China, combined with a tariff environment the company describes as "highly uncertain," and (3) a leveraged balance sheet built partly through acquisitions, with a large goodwill base and a pending acquisition of Masimo Corporation. The filing does not include segment revenue, margins, cash flow or peer data, so the assessment below is limited to what the risk factors disclose. Where I compute a ratio, it is labeled as illustrative.

## Risk 1: Healthcare Pricing and Reimbursement Pressure

The filing states that the healthcare industry "has undergone, and is in the process of undergoing, significant changes in an effort to reduce (and increase the predictability of) costs." It names the Affordable Care Act (PPACA), the Protecting Access to Medicare Act of 2014 (PAMA), which introduced a multi-year pricing program for the Clinical Laboratory Fee Schedule, and the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022, which contains drug price negotiation and rebate provisions. It also cites China's volume-based procurement program as having "unfavorably impacted our revenues." On the customer side, the filing describes group purchasing organizations, integrated health networks and competitive bidding as tools that let payors "reduce the prices they are willing to pay" and possibly exclude vendors from important segments.

**Industry vertical risk.** The filing describes the company's markets as "intensely competitive" and subject to "increasing consolidation," and it says regulation "tended to become more stringent over time and can be inconsistent across jurisdictions." Medical device and diagnostics products also require FDA clearance, which adds time and cost to launches. The resource does not provide data on how these conditions compare with other industry verticals, so I cannot rank the company's healthcare exposure against peers on the basis of this filing alone.

**Cyclicality.** Reimbursement pressure is driven mainly by policy rather than the business cycle, so it does not follow the usual economic pattern. The filing does tie some demand to the cycle, noting that certain businesses face "cyclical dynamics as a result of factors such as inventory de-stocking, high interest rates and depressed funding levels for biotechnology companies." Reduced biotech funding "has reduced demand for certain of our products," and government research funding "has been, and may in the future be, adversely affected." The risk is therefore less a question of when the economy turns and more a question of whether reimbursement rules and purchasing practices tighten at the same time as demand softens.

**Risk quantification.** The filing does not quantify the revenue or margin effect of PAMA, the Inflation Reduction Act or China's procurement program. The only quantified exposures in this section are geographic: approximately 59% of 2025 continuing-operations sales came from outside the U.S., and approximately 11% came from China. Because reimbursement changes in China and elsewhere feed directly into that 59%, the healthcare pricing risk and the international risk overlap. The filing also states that when customers conclude that reimbursement is inadequate, "it can adversely impact the prices at which we sell our products," and that the company is "not always able to pass along cost increases."

**Downside protections.** The filing does not describe long-term contracts or pricing floors for healthcare customers. It does describe breadth across "a variety of markets" and a wide product range, which spreads reimbursement exposure across many end-users. Less than 5% of 2025 sales were made to the U.S. federal government, which limits direct dependence on a single public payor. These protections reduce concentration but do not insulate margins from a broad reimbursement change.

## Risk 2: International Exposure, China and Tariffs

With 59% of 2025 continuing-operations sales outside the U.S., and 11% from China, the company's results depend substantially on conditions abroad. The filing lists more than a dozen international risks, including tariffs, capital controls, "longer payment terms than are typical in the U.S.," and "unexpected changes in laws or regulatory requirements, including changes in tax laws." On tariffs, the filing states that the U.S. Supreme Court ruled in February 2026 that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act does not authorize tariffs, and that the administration announced plans to impose new tariffs under alternative authority. The filing says "the full impact... remains highly uncertain and subject to rapid change." The company has already implemented surcharges and price increases in response to tariffs on imported supplies.

**Industry vertical risk.** Trade and tariff exposure is a feature of companies that manufacture across borders and sell widely abroad. The filing indicates that many manufacturing operations, suppliers and employees are outside the U.S., and that the growth strategy depends on penetrating "high-growth markets." That strategy increases the share of earnings exposed to policy decisions in countries where the company has less ability to enforce intellectual property rights. The resource does not contain peer comparisons, so this exposure cannot be ranked against other industries.

**Cyclicality.** Non-U.S. demand has its own cycles, which the filing ties to "devaluation of particular currencies," "military conflict," and "global economic uncertainty." The filing also notes that a stronger U.S. dollar "increases the effective price of our products sold in U.S. dollars into other countries" and produces unfavorable translation effects on non-U.S. results. Currency and economic cycles therefore compound the policy risk.

**Risk quantification.** The filing does not quantify the dollar cost of tariffs or the earnings effect of the China exposure. The directly quantified figures are these. Non-U.S. sales were approximately 59% of 2025 continuing-operations sales, and China was approximately 11%. Russia, Ukraine and Israel combined accounted for less than 1% of sales. As an illustrative calculation, if China's roughly 11% share were to fall by a quarter because of policy action, the sales effect would be about 2.75% of total continuing-operations sales, before any offsetting pricing or cost actions. The filing does not provide the inputs needed to convert that into an earnings figure.

**Downside protections.** The filing points to geographic breadth, with the U.S. accounting for roughly 41% of sales. It also notes that the company has suspended product shipments to Russia except for diagnostic, treatment and vaccine-related products, and that its sales to Russia, Ukraine and Israel are immaterial. Those steps limit exposure to specific conflict zones but do not reduce exposure to China.

## Risk 3: Leverage and Acquisition-Dependent Growth

The filing reports approximately $18.4 billion of outstanding indebtedness as of December 31, 2025, and approximately $3.9 billion of additional borrowing capacity. It says the company "expects to incur debt to finance a portion of the purchase price for our pending acquisition of Masimo Corporation." Net carrying value of goodwill and other intangible assets was approximately $61.0 billion at the same date. The filing states that the company "over the past several years" has not completed acquisitions "at rates similar to our historical practice," and that acquisitions can lead to "under-perform[ance] relative to our expectations and the price that we paid."

**Industry vertical risk.** Acquisition-led growth is common among diversified life sciences and healthcare tools companies, and the filing describes a portfolio built through "acquisitions, investments, joint ventures and other strategic relationships." The risk is that the growth model depends on continued access to affordable capital and on successful integration. The filing names "high valuations, competition among prospective buyers" and "instability in the credit markets" as constraints. The resource contains no peer leverage data, so the company's leverage cannot be compared with other industries here.

**Cyclicality.** This risk is directly rate- and credit-cycle sensitive. The filing notes that "increases in interest rates" could raise borrowing costs, that a credit downgrade "may" restrict access to debt markets on favorable terms, and that "significant negative industry or economic trends" can trigger goodwill impairment. The filing also states that impairment could result from "market capitalization declines" or "increases in associated discount rates." In a downturn, the same conditions that raise the cost of debt can reduce the fair value that supports the goodwill balance.

**Risk quantification.** Two illustrative ratios can be computed from the disclosed figures, with the caveat that they are not standard covenant measures. Debt of $18.4 billion relative to goodwill and intangibles of $61.0 billion is about 0.30 times (illustrative). Remaining borrowing capacity of $3.9 billion is about 21% of outstanding debt (illustrative). The filing also states that the company's credit facilities require it "to not exceed a specified, consolidated leverage ratio," but the threshold is not disclosed in the resource. The filing does not quantify any impairment charge, though it says the company "recognized impairment charges relating to certain non-goodwill intangible assets" in 2025 and prior periods.

**Downside protections.** The filing identifies several mitigants: the company's borrowing capacity under existing facilities, its credit ratings (which it works to keep, though the filing says they may be downgraded), and its practice of using purchase-price adjustments, earn-outs and indemnities in acquisitions. The filing also reports that the resolution of contingent liabilities tied to past divestitures "has not had a material effect" on the business, while stating that "there can be no assurance that this favorable pattern will continue."

## Nuanced Assessment

The three risks are connected. A reimbursement change in China or Europe affects the 59% of sales made abroad, a tariff change affects the cost of imported inputs and the price of exported products, and a weaker earnings base raises the cost of the debt used for acquisitions. The filing presents each risk separately, but the combined effect could be larger than any one of them alone, and the filing does not quantify that interaction.

The filing's own tone suggests that the most immediate risk is policy-driven rather than cyclical. The company describes the tariff outcome as "highly uncertain," and the Supreme Court ruling leaves open which replacement tariffs will apply. Reimbursement reform is also described as ongoing, with PAMA's effect "still unclear." These are risks whose timing the company cannot control, and the filing gives no estimate of their size. An investor relying on this document cannot separate the earnings effect of each risk from the others.

The leverage risk is the one most directly under management's control, and the filing gives the most specific figures for it. Management can choose the pace of acquisitions and debt issuance, but the pending Masimo transaction is already in progress and will add debt. The filing acknowledges that acquisitions have recently occurred at a slower pace than the company's historical practice, which suggests that the balance sheet has been more constrained in recent years than in the past. The filing does not explain the reasons behind that slower pace, so I cannot attribute it to a specific cause.

The downside protections are real but partial. Geographic breadth, a broad product range, low direct exposure to the U.S. federal government, and available borrowing capacity all reduce the chance of a single event causing severe damage. The resource does not disclose long-term supply or customer contracts, pricing mechanisms or hedging programs, so the protections that can be verified from the filing are structural rather than contractual.

What would prove this assessment too cautious: the Masimo transaction closing without a material increase in leverage, a clear tariff regime that the company can price through without margin loss, and evidence in later filings that reimbursement changes in China and the U.S. have not reduced volumes. What would prove it too optimistic: a credit downgrade, a goodwill impairment charge that goes beyond the non-goodwill intangible impairments disclosed in 2025, or further deterioration in China's procurement pricing that reduces the company's revenue from that market.
