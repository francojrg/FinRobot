# Top Three Key Risks in ASML's 2025 Risk Factors

The three risks that matter most in the filing are customer concentration combined with dependence on customer capital spending, export controls and geopolitical exposure, and a single-source supply chain anchored by Carl Zeiss SMT. Customer concentration rose sharply in 2025. The largest customer accounted for 23.9% of total net sales (€7,796.7 million), up from 16.6% (€4,682.4 million) in 2024, and the two largest accounted for 38.0%. Lithography system unit shipments fell over the same period, from 449 units in 2023 to 418 in 2024 and 327 in 2025. The filing does not quantify the three risks in financial terms, so the figures below are the filing's own disclosures, and any derived numbers are marked as illustrative.

## Risk 1: Customer Concentration and Dependence on Customer Capital Spending

Most of ASML's revenue comes from a small number of lithography systems sold to a small number of customers. The filing says that the loss of any key customer, or a substantial change in its purchasing behavior, could have a material adverse effect. Concentration is growing, not shrinking. The largest customer's share of net sales increased by about 7.3 percentage points in one year. Illustratively, that customer's sales rose roughly 66% (€7,796.7 million against €4,682.4 million), while the company's unit shipments fell about 22% (327 against 418). The filing attributes the sharp rise in concentration to ongoing consolidation in the semiconductor manufacturing industry and does not give a more specific cause for the shift between years. Because the filing notes that the ranking of the largest customers may shift from year to year, the 2025 position should not be read as a permanent change in the customer mix.

Credit exposure follows the same concentration. The three largest customers accounted for €1,294.2 million, or 35.4%, of accounts receivable and finance receivables at December 31, 2025, down from €2,641.9 million, or 54.1%, a year earlier. The filing states that business failure or insolvency of one main customer could result in significant credit losses.

**Industry vertical risk.** The resource contains no peer or sector-wide data, so this section cannot rank ASML's exposure against other industries. Qualitatively, the filing describes an industry with a small number of very large buyers, which is a structural feature of the business rather than a company-specific choice. The filing also describes intense competition in both DUV and patterning applications, including from Canon, Nikon, Applied Materials and KLA, which means lost share at a single large customer would not be easily recovered from another source.

**Cyclicality.** The filing states that the semiconductor industry has historically been cyclical, that the timing, duration and volatility of cycles are difficult to predict, and that these cycles can have a significant impact on ASML. It notes that Logic and Memory customers exhibit different levels of cyclicality and different business cycles. Sales of lithography systems depend in large part on customer capital expenditure, and the filing reports that some customers' capital spending has declined compared with prior years and that customers have scaled back capacity additions. The cost side is also exposed. The filing says that because ASML significantly increased its organization in previous years, it would be difficult to adjust costs quickly in a downturn. Revenue recognition adds a timing effect: the company has used fast shipments, under which formal customer acceptance can delay revenue recognition, so period-to-period comparisons can shift even when demand is unchanged.

**Risk quantification.** The factors that drive this risk are the number of units shipped (327 in 2025), the share of the largest customer (23.9%), the combined share of the two largest (38.0%), the concentration of receivables (35.4% for the three largest at year-end 2025), and the reliance of installed base revenue on system sales, which the filing says can also be affected by export restrictions. The filing does not disclose margins or profitability by customer, so the earnings effect of losing one large customer cannot be estimated from the resource.

**Downside protections.** The resource identifies few contractual protections. It does not describe long-term supply agreements with customers, and it does not disclose backlog. The filing offers two partial offsets: the growing share of metrology, inspection and computational lithography revenue, although the filing notes that many of these customers overlap with lithography customers, and installed base services. Neither offset removes the core dependence on a few buyers.

**What would prove this view wrong.** A lower share for the largest customer in the next reporting period, combined with a recovery in unit shipments and a falling share of receivables held by the three largest customers, would indicate that the concentration risk is easing. Evidence that the largest customers are increasing capital expenditure without a further rise in their share would also weaken the case.

## Risk 2: Export Controls, Sanctions and Geopolitical Exposure

Export controls are now a significant constraint on sales, and they are tightening. The filing states that Dutch, US and other laws require export licenses for EUV systems, specific DUV immersion systems and some other products. It also describes US trade measures that require licenses for transactions with certain Chinese entities and limit support by US persons for non-US-origin items destined for advanced-node fabs in China. The filing states plainly that ASML does not control the licensing process or approval criteria, and that the restricted list may be expanded.

The regional mix shows how much the risk matters in practice. Customers in China represented 29.1% of 2025 total net sales, down from 36.1% in 2024. Taiwan rose to 25.5% of 2025 net sales from 15.4% in 2024. South Korea was 25.0% in 2025 and 22.7% in 2024. The filing does not explain the causes of these shifts. The reduction in China's share is consistent with the export restrictions the filing describes, but the filing does not attribute the change to them. The concentration of sales in three countries is itself a risk, since the filing highlights Taiwan's unique international political status and the possibility of conflict affecting North Korea and South Korea.

**Industry vertical risk.** The resource does not provide comparative regulatory data for other sectors. Within the semiconductor equipment industry, the filing describes ASML as subject to a wider and more restrictive set of export and sanctions rules than its principal competitors would face in the same jurisdictions, but the resource does not compare its exposure with those of Canon, Nikon, Applied Materials or KLA, so no ranking can be drawn from the text. The filing also notes that countries affected by controls may impose countermeasures, which could create conflicting regulations and legal liabilities.

**Cyclicality.** This risk is not cyclical in the usual sense. It is a policy risk whose timing is set by governments. The filing notes that geopolitical conditions could worsen industry cyclicality, for example if countries increase domestic capacity for self-sufficiency and create global overcapacity. It also notes that the drive for technological sovereignty is one of the influences on customer capital expenditure. Export restrictions therefore interact with the cycle in Risk 1, because a restriction can remove demand at the same time the cycle is weakening.

**Risk quantification.** The relevant figures are the regional shares of net sales (China 29.1%, Taiwan 25.5%, South Korea 25.0% in 2025), the number of restricted product categories, and the scope of US entity restrictions, which the filing does not quantify. Illustratively, if total net sales were near €32.6 billion, implied from the largest customer's 23.9% share, then China's 29.1% share corresponds to roughly €9.5 billion of 2025 sales. This is a back-of-the-envelope figure derived from the filing's percentages, not a disclosed number, and it should be read as an order-of-magnitude indication only.

**Downside protections.** The resource describes few protections. The company's diversification across regions is the most important mitigating factor, since no single country is more than about 30% of sales in 2025. The filing also mentions that the company is expanding into new markets such as India, though it notes that expansion carries its own regulatory, political and talent risks. The Dutch innovation box and US R&D credits affect the effective tax rate, as the filing describes, but they do not offset export or sanctions risk. The filing does not describe any government support or indemnity against export restrictions.

**What would prove this view wrong.** Routine approval of export licenses, a narrowing of the US restricted list, or a return of China's share toward its 2024 level without any offsetting decline in other regions would indicate that the restriction risk is receding. Conversely, a further expansion of restricted products or entities would confirm the view.

## Risk 3: Single-Source Supply Chain and Production Concentration

The filing describes a supply chain that depends on a few suppliers for key components, with the most critical dependency being Carl Zeiss SMT. The company states that Carl Zeiss SMT is its sole supplier of lenses, mirrors, illuminators, collectors and other critical optical components, under an exclusive arrangement. The filing states that the number of lithography systems ASML can produce is limited by this supplier's production capacity, and that if Carl Zeiss SMT terminated the relationship or could not maintain optics production over a prolonged period, ASML would effectively cease to be able to conduct its business. The company's sourcing principle is "single sourcing, dual competence," which describes a supplier base where most parts have one source, with internal capability intended to allow a substitute to be qualified. The filing states that multi-sourcing is often economically impractical for EUV systems.

Production is also concentrated. All manufacturing, including subassembly, final assembly and system testing, takes place at eight facilities: Veldhoven, Eindhoven and Oirschot in the Netherlands, Berlin, Wilton and San Diego, Pyeongtaek in South Korea, and Linkou and Tainan in Taiwan. The filing states that alternative production capacity may not be available in the event of a major disruption. Most key suppliers also operate a limited number of facilities. The filing lists several causes of disruption, including labor strikes, fires, energy shortages, cyberattacks, blockades and climate-related events. It cites recent operational disruptions at some suppliers from raw-material shortages and cyberattacks. The company also notes that it recently began using ocean freight for some deliveries, which introduces transit risks.

**Industry vertical risk.** The resource does not compare supply concentration with other industries. Within its own sector, the filing describes the highly specialized nature of components as the reason single-sourcing has grown, which means the concentration is partly a function of technology rather than of commercial choice. Qualitatively, the filing presents this as a feature of the industry that the company cannot easily change.

**Cyclicality.** Supply risk is less directly cyclical than customer demand risk, but the filing links the two. It states that supply constraints can cause ASML to fall short of demand during upturns, while a decline in demand can leave it with overcapacity, higher costs and potential losses on investments in capacity. It also notes that increasing production requires suppliers to expand capacity and to invest, and that permits and construction time can delay expansion. The cyclical exposure therefore runs in both directions: under-supply in a boom and over-capacity in a downturn.

**Risk quantification.** The resource does not quantify the supply risk in monetary or unit terms. The relevant facts are the single source for optics, the eight manufacturing sites, the limited number of facilities at key suppliers and the lead-time increases the filing describes as a result of growing product complexity. The filing does not disclose inventory levels, supplier spending or the cost of a supply interruption, so no estimate of the earnings effect is possible from the resource.

**Downside protections.** The "dual competence" approach is a partial mitigation: the filing indicates that internal knowledge is maintained so that a substitute could be qualified, but the resource does not say how long qualification would take or whether a substitute exists for optics. The filing states that the company does not fully insure its risk exposure, that not all disasters are insurable, and that it may bear uninsured losses. It also states that the company and its suppliers are investing in expanding capacity, which is a mitigating action but not a protection in itself. The filing does not describe supply agreements that guarantee minimum volumes.

**What would prove this view wrong.** Qualification of a second source for critical optical components, or a documented reduction in the number of single-sourced parts, would show that the dependency is falling. Completed capacity expansions at Carl Zeiss SMT and other key suppliers, with no further supply constraints reported in subsequent filings, would also support a more favorable view.

## Overall Assessment

The three risks are connected. A downturn or a policy change that reduces demand from the largest customers would hit a cost base that the filing says is difficult to adjust quickly, while a supply constraint at Carl Zeiss SMT would limit the company's ability to recover the lost volume. Export restrictions can remove a region's demand at the same time the cycle weakens, and the filing's regional shifts show that the relevant exposures are moving, not stable. The resource gives little evidence of protections that would contain these risks together. It describes no long-term customer contracts, no quantified backlog, no supply guarantees and no insurance for the full range of exposures. Its strongest mitigating facts are geographic diversification across more than one region and the growing share of metrology, inspection and computational lithography revenue, but the filing does not quantify how much these offset the concentration.

The filing is also explicit that the list of risks is not exhaustive and that some risks may be considered immaterial today. The analysis here should therefore be read as a view of the disclosed exposures rather than a complete estimate of loss. The largest single uncertainty is whether the sharp fall in unit shipments from 449 in 2023 to 327 in 2025 reflects the cyclical pattern the filing describes or a structural shift in customer demand; the resource does not contain the information needed to decide between these explanations.
