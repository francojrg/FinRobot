# Visa Inc.: Top Three Risks from the Item 1A Disclosures

The three risks that matter most in this filing are regulatory pressure on the fees that drive Visa's economics, competitive and technological disruption that could reduce Visa's role in transaction processing, and cyber and operational failures that could create direct settlement losses. The regulatory risk is the most specific in the text, with named caps, dated settlements, and jurisdiction-level actions. The competitive risk is the broadest and hardest to bound. The cyber and settlement risk carries the most severe stated consequences, including the possibility that a failure of the retrospective responsibility plans could "cause us to become insolvent." The resource contains no financial tables, so none of the figures below are drawn from financial statements, and no revenue sensitivity can be calculated from this document alone.

## Risk 1: Regulatory Pressure on Interchange, Merchant Discount Rates, and Payments Sovereignty

Visa does not receive interchange revenue directly, but the filing states that interchange rates are "a key competitive factor that affects our overall payments volume and net revenue." The concrete exposures are a U.S. Federal Reserve cap on debit interchange for large issuers, with a proposed further reduction and automatic biennial adjustments; recent conflicting federal court decisions that "could result in a significantly lower interchange cap for debit transactions"; a European Interchange Fee Regulation that caps consumer credit and debit rates within the EEA; and a 2019 European Commission settlement that limits certain cross-border interchange rates and was extended through 2029. The Greek Parliament limited acquirer fees on certain small-ticket transactions for three years in 2024. The Credit Card Competition Act, which would require large issuers to offer routing choices for credit transactions, has been reintroduced, and the filing says it "remains unclear" whether it will advance.

The second part of this risk is sovereignty. In China, UnionPay is described as the predominant domestic processor, and Visa's application to operate a domestic Bank Card Clearing Institution was accepted in June 2020. The filing states that the PBOC approved Visa's Application to Prepare in "October 2026," which is a date that should be verified against the filing because it falls well after the 2020 acceptance and the surrounding text. Visa still needs further approval before it can begin domestic clearing, and the PBOC is considering phasing out dual-branded cards. Elsewhere, central banks are building real-time payment networks and digital currencies, and the European Central Bank has launched the European Payments Initiative's A2A system and a multi-year digital euro effort.

What would prove this risk overstated: a sustained pattern of fee caps that do not reduce payments volume, or a regulatory outcome such as the CCCA stalling without further action. The filing itself names the pattern to watch, which is whether regulatory developments "in one jurisdiction or product category may be replicated elsewhere or extended to other products."

## Risk 2: Competition, Disintermediation, and Client Concentration

The filing names competitors across several categories: closed-loop networks such as American Express and Discover, alternative systems such as Alipay and WeChat Pay, private-label networks, fintechs, technology companies, and government-backed real-time payment networks. It states that closed-loop models "may prove more adaptable to regulatory and technological developments." The GENIUS Act, enacted in July 2025, is described as providing "increasing regulatory certainty for stablecoins," which the filing says "could disrupt existing payment networks, particularly in cross-border, B2B, and currency-unstable markets." The filing also says industry participants "may withhold data used to power our solutions," which could reduce the effectiveness of Visa's value-added services.

Concentration is a second layer. The filing states that "a significant portion of our net revenue is concentrated among our largest clients" and that financial institution clients can terminate "on relatively short notice without paying significant early termination fees." The resource does not give the percentage of net revenue from the largest clients; the reader must consult Note 14 for that figure. Visa also pays for volume. The filing lists up-front cash payments, fee discounts, rebates, and performance-based incentives, and says continued pressure on these "could moderate our growth."

The filing's own framing gives the clearest test: if Visa cannot "anticipate or respond to the evolving competitive landscape," the consequence is harm to "business, financial results and prospects for growth." Evidence that the view is wrong would be stable or rising net revenue per transaction in markets where alternative systems have grown, or continued retention of the largest clients without rising incentive spending.

## Risk 3: Cyber, Operational, and Settlement Exposure

Visa's settlement guarantee exposes it to direct loss. The filing explains that Visa indemnifies issuers and acquirers for settlement losses caused by another institution's failure to honor settlement obligations, and that the exposure is "generally limited to the amount of unsettled Visa card payment transactions at any point in time." The filing also notes that, under Brazilian regulations, Visa may have to absorb certain settlement losses for transactions involving Brazilian sellers, and that enhanced operating rules submitted to the Central Bank of Brazil "are expected to increase our settlement exposure and related collateral requirements" once approved.

The cyber exposure is described in unusually specific terms. The filing lists attacks by governments against financial institution clients, phishing, deepfake schemes, distributed denial-of-service attacks, and zero-day exploitation. It also describes AI as cutting both ways: threat actors use GenAI and agentic AI to build "increasingly automated, targeted, coordinated" attacks, and Visa's own AI deployments "could expand our cybersecurity attack surface." Quantum computing is named as a future cryptography risk. The filing acknowledges that the company may not fully recover critical functions, that insurance "may not sufficiently cover all types of losses," and that third-party indemnification "may not be applicable or sufficient."

The filing's litigation disclosure links the two. It states that Visa is "required by some of our commercial agreements to indemnify other entities for litigation brought against them," and that the U.S. and Europe retrospective responsibility plans are the financial protections against certain litigation. Those plans have their own timing: the filing says the Europe series B and C preferred stock "will become fully convertible... no later than 2028," and that conversion of class B and C shares into class A common stock would dilute the voting power of existing class A shareholders.

## Industry Vertical Risk

The resource does not compare Visa's industry with other industries, and it contains no data on market volatility or the risk profile of other sectors. The filing does describe the payments sector as "intensely competitive and rapidly evolving" and as subject to "complex and evolving" regulation in multiple jurisdictions at once. On the text alone, the payments sector carries regulatory and technological risk that the filing describes as increasing, with the most concrete pressure coming from government action on fees and data. Any statement that payments is riskier or safer than another industry would have to rely on information outside this document.

## Cyclicality

The filing states that net revenue "is dependent on the volume and number of payment transactions made by consumers, governments and businesses whose spending patterns may be affected by economic, political, market, health and social events or conditions." It names recessions, inflation, rising interest rates, high unemployment, currency fluctuations, tariffs, and reduced consumer, small business, and corporate spending as conditions that would reduce volume. Cross-border exposure amplifies this: more than half of net revenue is earned outside the U.S., and the filing says that a decline in cross-border travel and spend "would reduce our cross-border volume... and... our international transaction revenue." The filing also says clients may cut marketing budgets and value-added service spending during downturns. The resource does not quantify how revenue has moved through past cycles, so the degree of cyclicality cannot be measured from this text.

## Risk Quantification

The figures available in the resource are as follows. More than half of net revenue is earned outside the U.S. The EC cross-border interchange settlement runs through 2029. The Europe preferred stock conversion deadline is 2028. The EU AI Act's key high-risk provisions take effect in 2027. Visa suspended its Russia operations in March 2022 and no longer generates revenue from Russia. Ownership limits are 15 percent of class A common stock and 5 percent for competitors. Shareholders need at least 15 percent of class A common stock, held for a year, to call a special meeting. The resource contains no revenue, margin, or volume totals, so the financial impact of any of these risks cannot be stated in dollar terms from this document.

## Downside Protections

Visa's protections are real but limited by the filing's own wording. Contractual protections exist, but the filing says clients "generally have flexibility to issue non-Visa products." Geographic diversification is substantial, since more than half of revenue comes from outside the U.S., though the filing ties cross-border exposure to the same macroeconomic and geopolitical risks. The settlement exposure is capped by design at the amount of unsettled transactions, and the retrospective responsibility plans provide financial protection against certain litigation. Insurance and third-party indemnification exist, but the filing explicitly says they may not be sufficient. Government regulation is a risk here rather than a protection, because the filing describes it as the main source of fee pressure. The resource does not describe the length or terms of client contracts beyond the termination language.

## Overall Assessment

The filing's risks are concentrated in three areas that interact. Regulators are reducing the fee base, sovereignty measures are shrinking the network's reach in some markets, and new payment forms such as stablecoins and agentic commerce could move transactions outside Visa's network. Each of these would reduce volume, and volume is the basis of Visa's revenue. The cyber and settlement exposure adds a direct balance-sheet risk that does not depend on volume. The filing's most important caveat is that many of its risks are described as uncertain in timing and magnitude: the CCCA may not advance, the Brazilian rules are not yet approved, and the stablecoin effects are "difficult to predict." The view in this summary would be proven wrong if regulatory caps were enacted without a measurable effect on volume, if the largest clients remained with Visa while alternative systems grew, or if the Brazilian and cyber-related exposures remained small relative to the protections described. Each of those outcomes can be checked against the financial statements and Notes 12, 14, and 20, which the resource refers to but does not include.
