# Palantir Technologies Inc.: Top Three Key Risks from the Form 10-K Risk Factors

Three risks in the filing matter most for an investor: customer concentration and contract termability, dependence on government budgets and fiscal timing, and the long, uneven path from sales pilot to recognized revenue. The excerpt does not include the consolidated financial statements, so revenue totals, margins, and cash flows cannot be quantified here. Every figure below comes from the risk factor text.

## Risk 1: Customer Concentration and the Right of Customers to Reduce or Exit

The filing states that "our top three customers together accounted for 16% and 17% of our revenue for the years ended December 31, 2025 and 2024, respectively." The concentration fell by one percentage point, so it has not worsened, but it remains a material share held by three relationships. The filing adds that these three customers "have been with us for an average of ten years," which suggests the concentration reflects long-standing relationships that have expanded over time rather than recent wins. The filing also discloses that certain large customers "have in the past reduced, and others may choose in the future to reduce, their spend with us or terminated their agreements," and that this has required "us to refund some previously paid amounts."

**Industry vertical risk.** The filing does not provide benchmark data comparing Palantir's customer concentration with other software or government-services businesses, so a direct ranking is not possible from these resources. Qualitatively, the risk factors describe large customers as holding "increased leverage... in negotiating contractual arrangements," and say that large enterprises and government entities "demand that vendors take on a larger share of risks." That combination suggests the concentration risk sits at the commercial and government contracting level rather than in the product itself.

**Cyclicality.** The filing ties renewal and expansion decisions to "general economic conditions," "reductions in our customers' spending levels," and macroeconomic factors including interest rates, foreign currency movements, and tariffs. Because the top customers are large organizations, a spending reduction at one of them would show up in a visible share of revenue. The filing notes that customers "may choose... to reduce" spend, which implies that the cyclical exposure runs through expansion revenue more than through the base of existing contracts.

**Risk quantification.** The disclosed figures are these: the top three customers at 16% of revenue in 2025 and 17% in 2024; total remaining deal value of $11.2 billion at December 31, 2025, of which $6.8 billion relates to commercial customers and $4.4 billion to government customers. Illustratively, the commercial share is about 61% of remaining deal value and the government share about 39% (simple division of the disclosed amounts). The filing does not state what portion of the $11.2 billion belongs to the top three customers, so the concentration in deal value cannot be measured directly from the excerpt.

**Downside protections.** The filing says "we generally offer contract terms of one to five years in length," and that the top three customers have been with the company for about a decade. Against this, the filing is explicit that "many of our customer contracts permit the customer to terminate their contracts with us with notice periods of varying lengths," and that customers "have no obligation to renew." Termination for convenience weakens the protection that contract length would otherwise provide. The protection that does exist is largely behavioral, namely the tenure of the largest relationships and their history of expansion, not a contractual floor.

## Risk 2: Dependence on Government Budgets, Appropriations, and Contract Options

The filing reports $4.4 billion of remaining deal value from government customers at December 31, 2025. It warns that "the U.S. federal government is prohibited from exercising contract options more than one year in advance," that "delays in the completion of the U.S. government's budgeting process, the use of continuing resolutions, and a potential lapse in appropriations... have and could in the future adversely affect our ability to timely recognize revenue," and that the company may "fail to receive and maintain government contracts or there may be changes in the contracting or fiscal policies of the public sector." These are named, specific mechanisms, and the filing says they have already affected revenue timing in the past.

**Industry vertical risk.** Government work carries a different risk profile from commercial software. The filing describes government customers as subject to "fiscal year end procurement cycle," with the U.S. federal fiscal year ending September 30. Government contracts also bring "security clearances," "government pricing guidelines," and potential "congressional, regulatory, or other government investigations or inquiries." The filing states that the company's relationships with government and sensitive-industry customers "have resulted in public criticism." On the other side, the filing does not describe government spending as more volatile than commercial spending; it describes government spending as set on a calendar that the company can partly anticipate.

**Cyclicality.** The filing identifies "a decline in the U.S. and other government budgets, changes in spending or budgetary priorities, or delays in contract awards" as a standalone risk. It also says that the company's expectation of growth from "increased government spending on technology aimed at national defense, financial or policy regulation, cybersecurity, or healthcare mandates" may "drive customer demand at different times throughout our year." The cyclicality is therefore tied to appropriation cycles and political priorities rather than to the broader economy alone.

**Risk quantification.** The disclosed figures are the $4.4 billion of government remaining deal value, the September 30 federal fiscal year end, and the rule that options cannot be exercised more than one year in advance. The filing does not split revenue between government and commercial customers in the excerpt, so the government share of recognized revenue cannot be stated. An illustrative sensitivity is possible but would require revenue data that the excerpt does not contain, so it is omitted here.

**Downside protections.** The filing describes a large contracted backlog (the $4.4 billion of government deal value) and multi-year contract structures. Deal value, however, is not a guarantee: the filing says "we historically have not realized all of the revenue from the full deal value of our customer contracts." Government contracts are also subject to termination for convenience. The protection that can be identified is the scale of the remaining government deal value and the fact that the company's growth strategy explicitly includes government mandates across several categories, which diversifies demand across agencies and purposes, though the filing does not quantify that diversification.

## Risk 3: Long and Unpredictable Sales Cycles and Quarterly Revenue Timing

The filing states that "our sales cycle often lasts six to nine months but can extend to a year or more for some customers." It describes a sales model in which the company provides "short-term pilot deployments... at no or low cost initially," and says that "there is no guarantee that we will be able to convert customers from these short-term pilot deployments to longer-term revenue-generating contracts." The filing also says the company "typically close[s] a large portion of our sales in the last several weeks of a quarter," and that because "a substantial portion of our expenses are relatively fixed in the short term," results "would suffer if revenue fell below our expectations in a particular period." Management has also said that "our operating expenses will continue to increase," and that the company "may not be able to maintain profitability in the future."

**Industry vertical risk.** The filing compares the company's enterprise and government sales exposure with the smaller customers it serves, noting that large organizations "typically have longer implementation cycles," "demand that vendors take on a larger share of risks," and "sometimes require acceptance provisions that can lead to a delay in revenue recognition." The filing does not benchmark the sales cycle against other software companies, so a relative ranking cannot be drawn from these resources. What the filing does establish is that the company's model front-loads costs (pilot deployments, direct sales hiring, and research and development) while revenue is "generally recognized over the contract term."

**Cyclicality.** The seasonality discussion indicates that "the first quarter of our year generally has relatively lower sales, and sales generally increase in each subsequent quarter with substantial increases during our third and fourth quarters." The filing links this pattern to government fiscal calendars, commercial budgeting cycles, and summer slowdowns in the United States, Europe, and other regions. It also says the company's rapid growth "may obscure the extent to which seasonality trends have affected our business." The cyclical exposure is therefore an intra-year pattern rather than a multi-year business cycle, and the filing expects it to "continue to materially impact our business."

**Risk quantification.** The disclosed figures are a six-to-nine-month typical sales cycle, one year or more for some customers, a "large portion" of sales closing in the last several weeks of each quarter, and the company's acknowledgement that large individual sales "have, in some cases, occurred in quarters subsequent to those we anticipated, or have not occurred at all." The filing also notes the company employs 4,429 full-time people as of December 31, 2025, and that expansion into new countries "requires additional setup and upfront costs that we may not recover." The filing does not disclose the amount of pilot-deployment spend, the conversion rate from pilots to contracts, or the size of operating expenses, so these cannot be quantified.

**Downside protections.** The filing identifies the contract structure as a partial buffer: because revenue is recognized over the contract term, "a decline in new or renewed contracts in any single quarter may have an immaterial impact on the revenue that we recognize for that quarter." The company has also "historically billed and collected payments for multiple contract years from certain customers in advance," though the filing says it has "shifted... to collecting payments on an annual or other basis, including in arrears." The filing reports that the company holds an undrawn $500.0 million revolving credit facility maturing in March 2027, and states that it "currently anticipate[s]" that operating cash flows and available funds will be sufficient for at least the next twelve months. This is management's expectation, not a guarantee, and the filing notes that the facility is secured by substantially all assets and carries covenants. It also states that a portion of cash is pledged as collateral for letters of credit and bank guarantees, and that this cash "cannot be used for our operations."

## Overall Assessment

The three risks are connected. The customer base that gives the company its long-tenured revenue is also the base that can reduce or terminate spend, and much of its contracted value comes from government customers whose purchase timing depends on appropriations and fiscal calendars. The sales cycle sits in front of both, so a slowdown in new large deals would show up in quarterly results before it shows up in recognized revenue, because the company recognizes most revenue over multi-year terms. The filing is candid that this combination has already produced results that fell short of expectations, citing the historical pattern of large sales arriving late or not at all and the history of refunds to customers who reduced spend.

The strongest mitigating facts in the excerpt are the length of the largest relationships, the scale of remaining deal value at $11.2 billion, the modest 1 percentage point decline in top-three concentration, and the undrawn credit facility. These reduce the probability of a sudden liquidity event. They do not remove the earnings risk, because the filing states that the company may not "maintain profitability in the future" and that its operating expenses are expected to keep rising.

The view in this assessment would be wrong if the top-three share returned to 17% or higher while total deal value stayed flat; if renewals shifted to shorter terms or reduced scope, which the filing lists as a risk; if the company's share of government deal value began to be recognized more slowly than its historical pattern; or if quarterly results became less concentrated in the final weeks of a quarter. Any of these would indicate that the sales cycle and renewal risks are moving from the risk factor language into reported results.

The governance point in the filing deserves a separate note. The filing states that the multi-class structure and voting agreements "concentrate voting power with certain stockholders," including Stephen Cohen, Alexander Karp, and Peter Thiel. This affects shareholders' ability to influence the three risks above, because the filing describes the company's decisions to forgo certain customers or markets, including China, as reflecting leadership's "mission and values." Those choices are described as potentially limiting growth, and the filing gives shareholders limited ability to change them through voting.
