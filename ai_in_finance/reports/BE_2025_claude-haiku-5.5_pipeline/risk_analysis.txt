# Top Three Key Risks for Bloom Energy Corporation

This assessment draws only on the Item 1A risk factors. The excerpt contains no financial statements, segment data, customer concentration percentages, or industry benchmarks, so the analysis cannot quantify revenue exposure, margins, or how Bloom compares with other industries on measured metrics. Where the comparison or cyclicality points below rely on judgment, they are labeled as such and rest on the language of the filing.

## Risk 1: Dependence on government incentives and tax equity financing

**Industry vertical risk.** The filing does not benchmark Bloom against other industries. Qualitatively, Bloom's exposure is high relative to a conventional equipment seller. Its products are priced and financed around tax benefits. The filing states that U.S. tax equity investors "are generally entitled to substantially all of the project's tax benefits," and that Bloom's "capacity to offer our Energy Server systems through financed arrangements depends in large part on the ability of financing parties to optimize the tax benefits." The policy environment is also described as unsettled. The filing notes that incentives "may expire on a particular date, end when the allocated funding is exhausted, or be reduced or terminated," and that "the continuation of these programs and incentives depends upon continued political support."

**Cyclicality.** The filing does not describe a business-cycle pattern for incentives. The relevant cyclicality is political and legislative. The filing describes a sequence of changes within a short period. After December 31, 2024, fuel cell Energy Server systems running on natural gas or a non-zero carbon fuel became ineligible for the federal investment tax credit (ITC) under the Inflation Reduction Act (IRA), except for property safe harbored before that date. On July 4, 2025, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA) restored the ITC for applicable fuel cell property at a 30% rate through 2033 for projects beginning construction after December 31, 2025, and restored accelerated depreciation. The filing also says the financing market can contract in weak economic or credit conditions, which ties this risk to the broader economic cycle.

**Risk quantification.** The filing provides these specific data points:
- The ITC rate of 30% applies through 2033 under the OBBBA, for projects beginning construction after December 31, 2025.
- The ITC was unavailable for natural-gas fuel cell systems placed under the IRA after December 31, 2024, except for safe-harbored property.
- California's fuel cell net metering (FC NEM) tariff was available for new installations only until December 31, 2023. Existing customers must meet greenhouse gas standards to stay on it.
- The filing names specific causes for past difficulty raising tax equity: "limited operating history at a large scale, historical lack of profitability and that we are the only party who can manufacture our products."
- Bloom must accurately interpret IRA and OBBBA requirements, including prevailing wage, apprenticeship, domestic content, "energy community" siting, and "prohibited foreign entities" rules. A failure could cut the federal tax benefit in part or in full, and customers or equity investors "may require us to indemnify them for certain of such reductions."

The filing does not state what share of Bloom's sales or backlog depends on these benefits, so the dollar magnitude of this risk cannot be determined from the resource.

**Downside protections.** The filing offers several partial protections. Non-U.S. markets provide some diversification of incentives: the filing names Korea and Europe, and it notes that Korea's Renewables Portfolio Standards were replaced in 2023 by the Clean Hydrogen Portfolio Standard (CHPS), which Bloom does not initially expect to require 100% hydrogen. The OBBBA restored the ITC at a 30% rate through 2033, which gives a defined window. Existing FC NEM customers can remain on the tariff if they meet the emissions standard. The filing also says that "the loss of FC NEM has not yet impacted our ability to sell our Energy Server systems for use in California." These are partial mitigants. Each depends on terms that the filing describes as subject to change.

## Risk 2: Financing-dependent demand, long sales cycles, and concentration in AI data centers

**Industry vertical risk.** Bloom's demand is tied to large, financed, multi-month projects, which is a higher-risk profile than selling standard equipment. The filing says that sales cycles are "typically 8 to 12 months but can vary considerably," that installation takes "between six to twelve months or more from the entry into a sales contract," and that "AI and data center customers and other large loads, tend to have longer sales cycles." The filing also says that "distributed energy generation and hydrogen production are still emerging markets." Without benchmark data, the comparison with other industries rests on the filing's description of an emerging market with long cycles and project-level financing.

**Cyclicality.** The filing ties Bloom's sales cycle directly to macroeconomic conditions. It cites "inflation, rising interest rates, availability of capital, a recessionary environment, geopolitical instability" as factors that can extend evaluations. It also notes that rising interest rates or "tightening of the supply of capital" could make financing harder. Because sales recognition depends on delivery and installation, a slowdown in customer decisions would reach reported revenue with a lag of several quarters. The filing says that "a small fluctuation in the timing of the sales transaction's completion could cause our operating results to vary materially from period to period."

**Risk quantification.** The filing supports the following:
- Sales cycle of 8 to 12 months, and installation of 6 to 12 months or more after contract signing.
- Revenue is not recognized on product sales until delivery or complete installation. The filing states that "even relatively short delays" can produce "a significant shortfall between the revenue we expect to generate in a particular period and the revenue that we are able to recognize."
- Bloom's AI customer share is rising. The filing describes "a significant increase in demand for Bloom Energy Server systems to meet the power needs of AI data centers" and lists the factors that could slow that demand, including "a deceleration in AI adoption," "local moratoria, protests or siting restrictions," and "improved grid interconnection timelines."
- The filing says Bloom's "ability to deploy our backlog is directly tied to our ability to secure project financing."
- Post-order cancellation is an additional exposure. Bloom uses cancellation fees, but the filing says these "may be unable to recover some, or all, of our costs."

The filing does not give AI customer revenue share, backlog size, or the percentage of projects that are financed. Those numbers would be needed to quantify this risk.

**Downside protections.** The filing describes several mitigants. Bloom offers multiple financing structures, including third-party arrangements, leases, and PPAs, so customers can use products without a direct purchase. Cancellation fees are sized "both to discourage customer cancellations and to defray our costs." Bloom sells across several regions, and the filing lists Korea and Europe as incentive markets alongside the U.S. These protections are described qualitatively. The filing offers no evidence of how well they have performed in a downturn.

## Risk 3: Regulatory and permitting exposure for natural-gas-fueled systems

**Industry vertical risk.** The filing reports a materially higher regulatory burden than many equipment sellers face, and the risk is concentrated in one product line. Bloom's natural gas Energy Server systems are exposed to rules on fossil fuel use, carbon emissions, and gas interconnection. The filing says that "most existing environmental laws and regulations preceded the introduction of our innovative fuel cell technology and were adopted to apply to technologies existing at the time (i.e., large coal, oil, or gas-fired power plants)." Few of the other risks in the filing describe a regulatory framework that is itself unsettled and inconsistent across jurisdictions in this way.

**Cyclicality.** Regulatory change in the filing is described as a policy trend, not an economic cycle. Certain municipalities "have banned or are considering banning new interconnections with gas utilities." The filing notes that "some jurisdictions are limiting new gas interconnections, although others are allowing new gas interconnections for non-combustion resources." The direction of regulation has been toward tighter rules on fossil fuels. The filing also describes shifts in customer procurement: "our customers' and potential customers' energy procurement policies may prohibit or limit their willingness to procure our natural gas-fueled Energy Server systems."

**Risk quantification.** The filing supports these data points:
- Permit timelines "have increased," and the level of certainty around issuance "has decreased."
- Bloom has seen "a reluctance in certain areas to issue permits for natural gas Energy Server systems," and where permits are issued, conditions have included "a requirement to blend costly renewable fuels."
- Under California's FC NEM regime, installations must meet greenhouse gas standards to stay on the tariff. The filing says that if fuel cells "cease to operate at a rate that is the same or better than the grid resources they are displacing, this may result in increased cost."
- The filing names a specific project-level cost: "utility companies commonly charge fees to industrial customers for disconnecting from the electric grid," and those fees "could change, thereby increasing the cost to our customers."
- Bloom is also exposed to rules on carbon pricing and emissions reporting. Its current generation "does produce some CO2."

The filing does not measure how many projects have been delayed or repriced because of permits, and it gives no dollar cost of compliance.

**Downside protections.** The filing gives some partial protections. Several jurisdictions allow new gas interconnections for "non-combustion resources like our Energy Server systems," and the filing states that Bloom's systems can operate on hydrogen or biofuels. The filing also notes that Bloom's systems "generally produce fewer carbon emissions than other onsite high power density generation and the average U.S. marginal power generation sources that our projects displace," which is a central argument for their acceptability. These are possible routes around the regulatory risk. They do not remove it, and the filing states that "the availability and/or current high cost of those natural gas alternatives in a particular location may make them less attractive to potential customers."

## Overall assessment

Taken together, these three risks are linked. Bloom's demand depends on customers who can finance long, multi-stage projects. Those projects depend on tax benefits that have changed twice in a short period, and on permits and interconnections that are becoming harder to obtain for natural gas systems. The filing describes the AI data center market as a major new source of demand, and it also names the conditions that could reverse that demand, including a slowdown in AI adoption, financing constraints, and local siting restrictions. A single adverse change in financing or permitting could delay several large projects at once, and revenue recognition on delivery or installation would carry that delay into reported results.

The filing's own text lists the most likely causes of a shortfall: the cost and availability of financing, the pace of permitting and interconnection, and changes in federal tax rules. It also names the conditions under which this assessment would be wrong. If IRS guidance under the OBBBA is clear enough that tax credit financing proceeds without delay, the financing risk in Risk 1 would be smaller than described. If the AI data center build-out continues at the pace the filing anticipates, and if permitting for natural gas systems stabilizes in the jurisdictions where Bloom sells, the demand and regulatory risks in Risks 2 and 3 would ease. If Bloom's 1 GW to 2 GW capacity expansion by the end of 2026 is completed on schedule, the production constraint noted in the filing would be reduced. The filing does not present data that confirms any of these outcomes, so the reader must judge them against later disclosures in the same filing, such as the financial statements and MD&A, which are outside this resource.
