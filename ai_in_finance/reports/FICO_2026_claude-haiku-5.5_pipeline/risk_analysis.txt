# Fair Isaac Corporation: Top Three Risks from the Fiscal 2026 Risk Factors

The filing points to three risks that matter most. The first is the displacement of FICO Scores in the U.S. mortgage market by VantageScore, which is already producing a sharp decline in the stock price. The second is concentration: 92% of fiscal 2026 revenues came from the banking industry, and the three major consumer reporting agencies are both key distributors and, now, direct sellers of a competing score. The third is competitive and technological pressure from AI, including customers building their own models, large language model developers, and pricing pressure, which bears on the Software segment's strategy around FICO Platform. The filing gives no revenue split by segment or product, so it does not let a reader size any of these risks in dollars. The analysis below relies only on the qualitative and dated disclosures in the risk factors.

## Risk 1: Erosion of FICO Score use in U.S. mortgages

**Industry vertical risk.** The filing describes the Scores segment as facing a regulatory risk that the other businesses do not share in the same form. Scores revenue depends on whether Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, the FHFA, and HUD continue to accept the FICO Score, and those agencies can change their rules by announcement. The filing names the sequence: in July 2025 the FHFA announced a "lender choice" regime; in April 2026 the Enterprises were to accept VantageScore from approved lenders; in September 2026 they were to accept it from all lenders; and in April 2026 HUD announced that from January 1, 2027 the FHA would also accept VantageScore and FICO Score 10T alongside the Classic FICO Score. The filing does not benchmark this against other industries, so it is not possible to say from the document whether this regulatory exposure is higher or lower than that of peers. It is clearly a risk set by a government counterparty rather than by market demand alone.

**Cyclicality.** The filing ties Scores volume directly to macroeconomic conditions, specifically "the volume of transactions in the U.S. mortgage and credit card markets," which it says account for a significant portion of Scores revenue. Mortgage origination volume therefore compounds the competitive risk: fewer transactions reduce the base of royalty-bearing events even if market share holds.

**Risk quantification.** The supporting facts are these. A significant portion of Scores revenue is attributable to the U.S. mortgage market, though no percentage is given. The company states that the stock price "has declined significantly" in the months after the FHFA and HUD announcements, but the filing gives no figures for the decline. The company also says the timing is uncertain: "It is difficult to predict the extent to which VantageScore will be adopted and used relative to FICO Scores." The filing lists additional constraints that could slow FICO's position even if the agencies keep accepting it, including delays in adopting FICO Score 10T, delays in supporting the FICO Mortgage Direct License Program announced in October 2025, and the pricing and business practices of the consumer reporting agencies.

**Downside protections.** The filing identifies few protections for this risk. The company mentions that the Enterprises still require "ongoing validation and approval" of the FICO Score, which means its position is contingent rather than secured. The FHA's inclusion of FICO Score 10T, if implemented, would keep the FICO brand in a second federal channel, but the filing presents this as one more source of competition, since VantageScore is also permitted there.

**Nuance.** The key uncertainty is the speed of adoption. The announcements show that the policy has moved from a single-score mandate to a choice regime and then toward broader acceptance of VantageScore, and the filing says the company cannot predict uptake. This view would be proved wrong if lender-level use of the FICO Score in Enterprise-delivered mortgages stays broadly stable through the FHA's January 2027 effective date and if the agencies do not extend VantageScore's acceptance beyond what has been announced.

## Risk 2: Concentration in banking and reliance on the consumer reporting agencies

**Industry vertical risk.** The filing states that 92% of fiscal 2026 revenues came from sales to the banking industry, which makes this a single-vertical business in the sense that matters for this risk. The filing also says that most revenue growth in banking has come from "selling and cross-selling our products and services to large banks and other credit issuers," and that the rate of account growth in U.S. banking "has been slow." Its customers are "significantly larger than we are" and "may have sufficient bargaining power to demand reduced prices and favorable nonstandard terms." Put together, the company has a concentrated customer base with limited growth in its core account base and buyers with leverage over price.

**Cyclicality.** This is the most explicitly cyclical of the three risks. The filing says that economic downturns, rising interest rates, geopolitical tension, tariffs, and inflation can reduce consumer demand for financial services, reduce the number of credit applications, and lead to "a decline in the volumes of products and services we provide." It adds that banking contraction, through mergers that combine separate contracts, can reduce per-transaction recurring revenue. The filing also says that a sustained slowdown could cause customers to "reduce their spending, renegotiate or terminate our agreements."

**Risk quantification.** The filing supplies these data points: 92% of fiscal 2026 revenues from banking; 20% of fiscal 2026 revenues from outside the U.S. The second figure implies that the revenue base is both domestically concentrated and concentrated in one vertical. As arithmetic only, the 92% banking figure leaves about 8% of revenues from other industries. The filing does not give the share of revenue from the three consumer reporting agencies, but it states that the company "derive[s] a substantial portion of our Scores segment revenues and operating income" from contracts with Experian, TransUnion, and Equifax. The filing also flags that these three distributors "have developed and are actively selling VantageScore to compete directly with our products." Two of the company's largest distribution relationships are therefore also its most direct competitors in the mortgage score.

**Downside protections.** The filing mentions long-term contracts and renewals, and notes that some revenue is recurring from per-transaction contracts. It also describes some diversification: the company sells scoring, fraud, customer communication, customer management, and decision management solutions, and roughly one-fifth of revenue comes from outside the U.S. None of these protections is quantified, and the filing notes that renewals and long-term contracts can be renegotiated on less favorable terms under competitive pricing pressure.

**Nuance.** The protection from diversification is limited because the non-banking and non-U.S. revenue shares are small relative to banking. The more important question is whether the consumer reporting agencies keep distributing FICO products while also selling a competing score. This view would be proved wrong if the company renews or extends its agreements with the three agencies at stable economics and if its banking customers continue to expand their use of the FICO Platform products despite the slow account growth the filing describes.

## Risk 3: Competition and AI-driven substitution in the Software segment

**Industry vertical risk.** The filing presents the competitive field as unusually broad. It lists 14 categories of competitors, from in-house data science teams and "developers and providers of large language models" to consulting firms, consumer reporting agencies, and ERP and CRM vendors. It also says many competitors have "greater financial, technical, marketing, professional services and other resources," and that industry consolidation is "creating even larger competitors." Customers may build their own scoring models "including through the use of AI-based offerings." This is a wider and less predictable field than the single-score competition in Risk 1.

**Cyclicality.** The filing ties Software segment results to the timing of customer decisions rather than directly to the economic cycle. It says sales cycles "may extend beyond one year," that purchases "typically involve a significant commitment of capital," and that customers "particularly those experiencing financial stress" may buy more cautiously. It also notes that "many are fixed and cannot be quickly adjusted as revenue changes" for operating expenses, which magnifies any shortfall. Economic weakness therefore affects this risk through the sales pipeline and the cost base at the same time.

**Risk quantification.** The filing supplies these data points: a workforce reduction plan that produced pre-tax charges of $27.3 million in the fourth quarter of fiscal 2026, "substantially all of which are expected to result in future cash expenditures"; a statement that the company has "experienced delays in developing and introducing new products" in the past, "primarily due to difficulties developing models, acquiring data, and adapting our products"; and a statement that the company has identified "errors or 'bugs'" in its products despite pre-release testing. The filing also cites the risk that AI-generated attacks and AI-related product defects could delay new products. The $27.3 million figure is not compared with any revenue or margin figure in the resource, so its size relative to the business cannot be assessed.

**Downside protections.** The filing describes the modular FICO Platform strategy as a way to sell "multiple connectable and extensible products" and to let clients expand usage over time. That structure can support revenue expansion within existing accounts. The filing also says the company has "commercial rights" to government-funded technologies, though the U.S. government retains ownership. The filing does not present these as protections against competition and states that the market "may be unreceptive" to the platform approach.

**Nuance.** The filing is candid that the company has had product delays and defects in the past and that its own AI use can create new errors. This is a risk of execution as well as of competition. The view would be proved wrong if the company reports growth in Software segment revenue that is not dependent on sales of new platform products, if product delays do not recur in the new AI-based releases, and if the company's pricing holds without concessions that the filing says could reduce margins.

## Overall assessment

The three risks are connected. The Scores segment is exposed to a regulatory change that the filing says can happen quickly through announcements. The company's largest revenue vertical, banking, is cyclical and has slow account growth. Its strongest distribution partners are now also competitors. Its growth strategy depends on a platform that the market may not adopt at the pace the company expects. The filing does not quantify how much revenue sits in each of these exposures, which limits the precision of any assessment. What the document does establish is that these exposures are present, that they have already affected the stock price, and that the company's own disclosures describe limited protection from contract structure or diversification. The question an investor needs answered is how much of the Scores segment revenue depends on the mortgage channel, and the filing does not supply that figure.
