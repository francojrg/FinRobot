The three risks that most shape S&P Global's downside, based on the risk factors provided, are: (1) cyber and information-security exposure, (2) regulatory and legal exposure, concentrated in the Ratings, Indices and Energy businesses, and (3) cyclicality of transaction-based and asset-linked revenue. The excerpt contains no segment revenue, margin, litigation reserve or cyber-spending figures, so the quantification below is limited to the thresholds the filing names. The filing also states that none of the cyber incidents or third-party failures it describes has had a material adverse effect to date, while stating that the volume of attacks is rising. The assessment therefore rests on forward-looking exposure rather than on realized losses.

### Risk 1: Cybersecurity and information security

**Industry vertical risk.** The filing ties this risk to the company's position rather than to a single segment. It says the company's size, prominence and possession of material non-public information (including on sovereign and corporate issuers and on its own benchmark methodology changes) make it a target for state-sponsored actors, and it notes that the threats have grown more sophisticated, including AI-assisted attacks. The excerpt does not compare this exposure with other industries, so any ranking relative to peers would be analyst judgment rather than a figure from the filing. What the filing does establish is that the risk attaches to all segments, since the data handled spans credit ratings, benchmarks, energy price assessments and automotive data, and the exposure extends to vendors, data partners and distribution partners.

**Cyclicality.** Cyber risk is largely independent of the economic cycle. The filing notes, however, that military conflicts increase cybersecurity incidents, which links this risk to the geopolitical events that also drive market volatility. Attack volume is therefore less cyclical than revenue, and a period of market stress may coincide with a higher incidence of attacks.

**Risk quantification.** The filing gives no dollar figure for cyber losses or for security spending in this excerpt. It states that the company "devote[s] significant resources" to security, expects to spend significantly more, and cannot guarantee that these measures will be effective. It also identifies specific gaps: fragmented security tooling that "could create visibility gaps," acquired entities with vulnerabilities discovered after closing, and reliance on third-party vendors for which "insufficient recourse" may exist. The filing also records that it has experienced past insider trading incidents involving employees. The consequences listed include regulatory action, litigation, customer attrition and uninsured losses.

**Downside protections.** The filing lists insurance, security investment and business-continuity planning, but it also says that some losses may be "either not insured against or not fully covered." Insurance is therefore a partial offset only. The strongest evidence of protection is the company's own statement that no cyber event has yet had a material effect. That is a record of past outcomes, not a structural protection, and it should be read as such.

### Risk 2: Regulatory and legal exposure

**Industry vertical risk.** This is the risk most concentrated by segment. The filing states that many proceedings "regularly relate to the activity of our Ratings, Indices, and Energy businesses." Ratings operates under the U.S. Credit Rating Agency Reform Act of 2006, the Dodd-Frank Act, the Exchange Act and the EU and U.K. credit rating agency regimes. Indices and Energy operate under the EU and U.K. Benchmark Regulations and IOSCO principles, and Indices is licensed and supervised by the Australian Securities and Investment Commission for the S&P ASX 200. The filing adds new ESG ratings regulation that will require FCA supervision of the S1 business from June 2028. Compared with the Mobility and Market Intelligence businesses, which the filing describes primarily in terms of demand and cost exposure, the Ratings, Indices and Energy businesses face direct supervisory obligations, which is a meaningful difference in risk profile. This comparison relies on the scope of the risk factor language rather than on any quantified measure.

**Cyclicality.** Regulatory risk is not cyclical in the usual sense. It is, however, event-driven and can change abruptly. The filing says that changes "happen abruptly, such as following a change in government," and that proposed rules could reduce demand for credit ratings or raise costs the company "may be unable to pass through to customers." Regulatory shocks can therefore coincide with, or amplify, a downturn in the businesses they affect.

**Risk quantification.** The filing supplies the following concrete thresholds. A failure to comply with the GDPR or U.K. GDPR could result in fines of up to the greater of €20 million (or £17.5 million under the U.K. GDPR) or 4% of annual global revenues. A DPPA violation could result in liquidated damages of $2,500 per individual's personal information. For sanctions, the filing states that "the Company has previously settled and paid fines" in connection with such matters, without giving amounts. Illustratively, because the 4% ceiling scales with revenue, every $1 billion of annual global revenue would imply a GDPR ceiling of $40 million; the filing's revenue figure is not in this excerpt, so this is not a company-specific estimate. The filing also declines to estimate litigation outcomes, stating that the company "cannot predict the eventual outcome" and "in most cases [cannot] reasonably estimate" judgments, and that it "may not have adequate insurance or reserves."

**Downside protections.** Regulatory frameworks create some barriers to entry, because licensing, supervision and benchmark governance raise the cost of entry for new competitors, and the filing notes that the company has aligned with IOSCO principles and obtains an annual independent review of Indices' adherence to them. These are real structural features. The same frameworks, however, are the source of the risk, because they add compliance cost and can be changed by policy. The filing's own language indicates that compliance raises costs, and that the company cannot be sure that such costs can be passed through.

### Risk 3: Cyclicality of transaction-based and asset-linked revenue

**Industry vertical risk.** Several businesses depend on market activity rather than on contracted recurring spend. The filing states that "a significant component" of credit-rating and issuance-based revenue "is transaction-based, and is essentially dependent on the number and dollar volume of rated debt securities issued." Market Intelligence revenue tied to debt and equity issuance is similarly exposed. Indices depends on asset levels, notional values and exchange-traded derivatives volumes. Mobility depends on new and used vehicle supply and demand and on automotive supply chains. Against this, the filing also describes subscription-based products, which are exposed mainly to customer headcount and budget reductions during recession. The filing does not rank these businesses by volatility, so the comparison is qualitative: transaction and asset-linked revenue is more directly volatile than subscription revenue.

**Cyclicality.** This risk is the most clearly cyclical of the three. The filing lists the drivers: high or rising interest rates and credit spreads, volatility in financial and energy markets, defaults by significant issuers, recession and inflation. It also notes that some fees "are based on the performance of our customers," so pricing can fall when customers' markets weaken. The filing's language on energy is more mixed: it says volatility "could cause reduced demand" for products in key markets, which implies that the Energy business is not simply a beneficiary of volatility. Taken together, the businesses are exposed to a common macroeconomic driver, which means diversification across segments offers less protection in a broad downturn than the number of segments might suggest.

**Risk quantification.** The filing does not quantify the sensitivity of revenue to issuance volumes, asset levels or interest rates in this excerpt. The qualitative exposures it identifies are: issuance-linked revenue falls when issuance falls; Indices revenue falls when asset levels or derivatives volumes fall; subscription revenue falls when customer headcount falls; and fees fall when customers' performance falls. The filing also names competitive pressure as an amplifier, stating that the company "may be required to significantly reduce the costs for our products or services" if competitors price lower, and that fee reductions are "driven" by market changes.

**Downside protections.** The subscription-based products and the diversified mix of segments offer partial protection. The filing's contractual protections are limited in this excerpt: it notes that some data-supplier agreements "allow them to cancel on short notice," which weakens the stability of the input side, and it does not describe multi-year customer contract terms. The protection against cyclicality therefore rests mainly on the mix of revenue, which the excerpt does not quantify.

### Overall assessment

The three risks are linked. A sharp market downturn reduces issuance and asset levels (Risk 3), which tends to increase the scrutiny of rating and index actions and the litigation that follows volatility; the filing states that such scrutiny "has in the past" affected its reputation and led to proceedings (Risk 2). Cyber exposure compounds both, because the filing notes that military conflicts and market stress raise the number of incidents and that stakeholders can respond to a breach by losing confidence (Risk 1). The filing's most candid point is that its legal, regulatory and cyber costs cannot be estimated with confidence, and it says so repeatedly.

Two other risks that the filing emphasizes deserve mention, though they are not in the top three. The first is AI and free information: the filing says that competitors deploying AI, and the growth of free public data, could "materially reduce demand," and that the company cannot yet predict which AI offerings will succeed. The second is dependence on third-party data suppliers and on AWS, which the filing says it runs "a significant amount of our computing on." Both could alter the cash flows of the segments discussed above.

The view in this assessment would be proven wrong by several observable developments. A major cyber event with material financial effect would show that the "no material adverse effect to date" record does not hold. A regulatory action or fine materially larger than the sanctions fines the filing discloses would show that the compliance framework is less protective than described. A sustained decline in issuance-linked revenue that is smaller than the decline in issuance volumes would show that the transaction exposure is less direct than the risk factors imply. Finally, quantified disclosures in the notes to the financial statements (litigation reserves, cyber costs and segment revenue mix), which are not in this excerpt, would be needed to test all three conclusions.
