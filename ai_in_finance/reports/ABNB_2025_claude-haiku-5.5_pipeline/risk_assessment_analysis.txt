# Airbnb, Inc.: Top Three Risks from the FY2025 Form 10-K Risk Factors

Airbnb's three largest risks are regulatory and tax exposure tied to short-term rentals and to its payments and insurance businesses, dependence on travel demand that moves with the economy and the season, and liability and insurance costs from safety incidents on the platform. The first is the only one with quantified exposures in the filing: a $1.3 billion U.S. tax proposal, a reserve the filing says is more than $1.0 billion short of that figure, and three Italian settlements totaling 894 million euros. The filing does not give peer comparisons or industry-wide data, so the "industry vertical" comparisons below are qualitative and drawn only from what the filing says.

## Risk 1: Regulatory and Tax Exposure

**Industry vertical risk.** The filing describes a regulatory environment that is "complex, evolving, and sometimes inconsistent" across more than 220 countries and regions. Airbnb's core product is subject to local rules on length of stay, host registration, permits, and night caps, and the filing states that these rules "have limited and may continue to limit the ability or willingness of new or existing hosts to list." Hotels and hotel-affiliated groups lobby for stricter rules, and the filing says these efforts are ongoing. Few consumer platforms face this combination of local land-use rules, platform-liability regimes, and tax-collection duties at the same time. The filing does not provide a comparison with other platform or hospitality companies, so the degree of difference is an inference from the filing's own description.

**Cyclicality.** Regulation does not follow the economic cycle. Its effects are episodic and tied to political decisions, but they can compound in a downturn, since the filing notes that the cost and complexity of compliance rise regardless of demand.

**Risk quantification.** The filing supplies several specific figures:

- **U.S. federal income tax (IRS).** The 2013 Notice of Proposed Adjustment proposed an increase to U.S. taxable income that could result in $1.3 billion of additional tax, plus penalties and interest. The filing states this "exceeds our current reserve ... by more than $1.0 billion." Illustratively, if the proposed tax is $1.3 billion and the shortfall is more than $1.0 billion, the reserve is below roughly $0.3 billion. This figure excludes penalties and interest. In May 2024 the IRS issued a Statutory Notice of Deficiency claiming the same $1.3 billion, and Airbnb petitioned the U.S. Tax Court in July 2024. The filing states that an adverse outcome "could require a significant cash payment."
- **Italy.** Airbnb Ireland paid 576 million euros ($621 million) to settle 2017–2021, 139 million euros ($150 million) for 2022, and 179 million euros ($186 million) for 2023. Together these total 894 million euros, or about $957 million at the dollar figures the filing gives. Airbnb Ireland began withholding host income tax on Italian listings in 2024, but the filing says audits on permanent establishment, transfer pricing, withholding, and tourist taxes remain possible.
- **Spain.** In 2025 the Spanish Ministry of Consumer Affairs proposed a fine of about 65 million euros ($76 million) for alleged non-compliance with short-term rental listing rules.
- **New York.** The filing says the City of New York "has effectively banned short-term rentals," and that similar restrictions are being considered across New York State.
- **EU.** The EU Short-Term Rental Regulation takes effect in May 2026 and will require additional transparency, registration, and data-sharing steps.
- **Payments.** Several payments-related regimes apply, including money transmission licensing in the United States, an EEA payments institution license in Luxembourg, and an electronic money institution license in the UK. The filing says non-compliance with EU AML rules could bring fines of up to 10% of Airbnb's Luxembourg payments entity's annual revenue.

**Downside protections.** Geographic spread limits the damage from any single jurisdiction. The filing states that 61% of 2025 revenue came from listings outside the United States, so a ban in one city or country does not represent the whole business. The company has also resolved some disputes by removing listings or sharing data, which the filing says reduces the risk of a larger penalty. The Italian settlements remove a known liability in those periods. The filing does not, however, offer assurance on future audits, and it notes that settlements may create precedents for other governments.

## Risk 2: Travel Demand, Seasonality, and Competition

**Industry vertical risk.** The filing says Airbnb's "financial performance is dependent on the strength of the travel and hospitality industries," and that leisure travel "forms a substantial part of our business." Discretionary travel is among the first categories consumers cut when confidence weakens, and the filing lists unemployment, consumer debt, inflation, tariffs, and financial market volatility as factors that reduce spending. Airbnb also competes with online travel agencies, search engines, hotel chains, property managers, and "super-apps," and the filing notes that many of these competitors have greater brand recognition, larger marketing budgets, and more resources.

**Cyclicality.** The filing states that "economic downturns have historically reduced travel spending," and that a downturn leads to "lower bookings, increased cancellations, and reduced revenue." The business is also seasonal, with a peak in the third quarter across North America and EMEA. Revenue is recognized at check-in, while bookings are taken earlier, so a sharp fall in bookings may not appear in reported revenue until later quarters. The filing says expense levels are based on revenue estimates and that fixed expenses "may lead to greater-than-expected losses if revenue falls short." This timing mismatch is the mechanism by which a demand shock reaches profit.

**Risk quantification.**

- Non-U.S. dollar revenue was about 54% of the total in 2024 and 56% in 2025, so a strong dollar reduces reported revenue. The filing notes that a hedging program exists but "may not fully mitigate losses."
- Airbnb's marketing depends on search and social channels. The filing describes rising keyword costs and changes to search algorithms as reasons customer acquisition costs could rise, and it names AI-driven search and the favoring of in-house services by Google or Apple as channels that could reduce traffic.
- The filing identifies no quantified demand sensitivity. A reader cannot derive a revenue decline in a recession scenario from the resources provided.

**Downside protections.** The company operates in more than 220 countries and regions and in about 50 currencies, and the filing notes that many hosts are paid in their local currency. A regional downturn therefore need not affect all markets equally. The filing also describes a Live and Work Anywhere policy and "other" revenue initiatives, but it does not quantify their contribution to revenue, so their value as protection cannot be measured from the filing.

## Risk 3: Trust and Safety Liability and Insurance Cost

**Industry vertical risk.** Airbnb's model rests on strangers entering each other's private property. The filing states that Airbnb "do[es] not independently verify" the safety, location, or legal compliance of all listings, and that it relies on user-reported issues, which "may be incomplete or inaccurate." The filing lists incidents involving alleged fatalities, injuries, sexual violence, carbon monoxide, and hidden cameras, and says claims have been asserted for these. Few consumer businesses carry this kind of physical-safety liability at this scale. Again, the filing does not provide a peer comparison.

**Cyclicality.** Incident frequency is not tied to the economic cycle. Insurance costs are, however, tied to loss experience and to market capacity. The filing says "due to the limited availability of companies insuring our risks," the company has had increasing difficulty obtaining appropriate coverage at reasonable cost.

**Risk quantification.**

- The Host Damage Protection program reimburses up to $3 million per qualifying loss.
- The filing says "spending for insurance has increased as our business has grown" and that "losses from covered claims have increased, resulting in increased premiums."
- The filing names six failure points in the insurance program, including claim costs exceeding the captive insurance subsidiary's reserves and claims exceeding coverage limits.
- Insurance costs are rising, but the filing does not disclose the dollar amounts of premiums, claims, or reserves, so the size of this exposure cannot be quantified from the resources.

**Downside protections.** Airbnb uses a combination of commercial insurance and self-insurance through a wholly-owned captive, which gives it some control over retained losses. Its host protection programs, including AirCover, are a stated part of the business model. The filing also notes that some insurance "may or may not be applicable to some claims," and that coverage may not be available in the future "on economically reasonable terms or at all." These protections therefore reduce risk without eliminating it.

## Overall Assessment

The three risks share a common root: Airbnb is a marketplace that makes money by taking a fee on transactions it does not own, in jurisdictions whose rules it does not control. The filing's language is consistent with this. Most of its risk factors describe what happens if a third party (a government, a host, a guest, an insurer, or a competitor) acts against the company's interests.

The regulatory and tax risk is the most quantifiable and, in the filing's own words, the most likely to cause a "significant cash payment." The $1.3 billion IRS claim is roughly equal to the company's own 2025 total of the stated exposures, and the filing says the reserve is well short of it. Airbnb says it "strongly disagree[s]" and will contest the claim, so the outcome is uncertain, and the filing does not give a probability. A reader should treat the IRS matter as the single largest identified contingency in the document.

The demand risk is less quantifiable from this filing but is structurally important. Revenue is recognized at check-in while costs are partly fixed, so a sudden fall in bookings compresses margins with a lag. Seasonality concentrates earnings in the third quarter, which makes any single quarter's result a weaker signal than the full-year trend.

The safety and insurance risk is a cost that grows with scale. The filing states that costs will rise as the business grows and expands into new offerings. This is a structural headwind to margins, although the filing does not quantify it.

Several facts in the filing cut against a simple reading of risk. The company has no borrowings under its $1.0 billion revolving credit facility, with $20 million of letters of credit outstanding, so liquidity is available beyond cash on hand. The Italian settlements, although large, resolved multiple audit years at once. Revenue is spread across more than 220 countries. These points reduce the probability that any one risk threatens solvency. The filing does not, however, say how the $2.0 billion of 0% convertible notes due March 2026 are to be repaid beyond the general statement that the company may be unable to pay, so the repayment plan should be checked against the balance sheet and liquidity sections of the full 10-K, which were not part of the resource.

**What would prove this assessment wrong.** On the tax side, an IRS settlement or Tax Court ruling close to the existing reserve would indicate that the exposure is smaller than the filing's language suggests, while a ruling for the IRS on the full $1.3 billion plus interest and penalties would confirm it as a material cash event. On the regulatory side, the EU STR Regulation's effect after May 2026 and any spread of New York-style bans to other cities would show whether local restrictions are becoming a systemic constraint on supply. On demand, a sustained decline in bookings that is not explained by seasonality or currency would indicate that the travel-cycle risk is larger than the filing's diversification suggests. On safety, a rise in insurance premiums or reserve charges faster than booking growth would show that the insurance cost risk is compounding. Each of these would be visible in future filings, and the company's own statements that it "may not fully succeed" in trust and safety efforts provide no reassurance against that outcome.
