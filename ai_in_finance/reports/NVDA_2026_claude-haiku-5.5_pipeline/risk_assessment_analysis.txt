# NVIDIA: Top Three Risks and Their Assessment

The three risks that matter most in NVIDIA's 10-K are (1) a mismatch between supply commitments and customer demand that has already produced large charges, (2) U.S. and Chinese government restrictions that have effectively removed the company from China's data center market, and (3) heavy dependence on a few direct customers combined with counterparty exposure from financing and capacity commitments. The first two have produced quantified losses in the filing. The third has not yet produced a loss, but the filing describes it as a growing exposure. The filing does not give revenue totals, segment profit, or peer data, so the comparisons below are qualitative and drawn only from the filing's own descriptions.

## Risk 1: Supply and demand mismatch, inventory, and purchase commitments

**Industry vertical risk.** The filing says NVIDIA builds finished products and holds inventory ahead of anticipated demand, and that it has "previously experienced and may continue to experience extended lead times of more than 12 months." It also states that non-cancellable and non-returnable purchase orders placed ahead of lead times amplify the risk. The filing does not compare this exposure with other semiconductor or technology companies, so I cannot say whether NVIDIA's commitment profile is higher or lower than its peers. What the filing does establish is that the company's exposure is structural: it sells products whose supply it commits to well before the end-demand signal arrives, and it has been adding prepaid and capacity agreements. The filing states that these commitments "have increased our product costs and this may continue."

**Cyclicality.** The filing ties demand to conditions that move in cycles: customer capital expenditure, data center and energy availability, and consumer spending on Gaming products. It lists "lower capital expenditures by businesses, including on IT infrastructure" and "lower consumer confidence and spending" among the adverse economic effects. It also gives a concrete example of a demand cycle driven by a single use case: the Ethereum 2.0 merge in 2022 "reduced and may in the future decrease the usage of GPUs for Ethereum mining," which created aftermarket sales that "could negatively impact retail prices for our GPUs and reduce demand for our new GPUs." The filing does not quantify how cyclical revenue has been.

**Risk quantification.** The filing provides these data points:

- In the second quarter of fiscal 2025, gross margins were "negatively impacted by inventory provisions for low-yielding Blackwell material." No dollar amount is given for this provision.
- The filing states that the company has had to "reduce average selling prices," "write down our inventory, incur cancellation penalties, and record impairments."
- The company has "paid premiums, provided deposits, and entered into long-term supply agreements and capacity commitments."
- The filing states that "these risks have increased and may continue to increase as our purchase obligations and prepaids have grown and are expected to continue to grow and become a greater portion of our total supply."
- The filing says that supply constraints are expected to be "a headwind to Gaming in the first quarter of fiscal year 2027 and beyond."

The filing does not state the size of total purchase obligations, so the ratio of those obligations to revenue cannot be calculated from the provided resources.

**Downside protections.** The filing offers limited protection here. Supply commitments are largely fixed in advance, and the filing states that the company may not "be able to reduce our supply commitments in time, at the same rate, or at all." Mitigants described in the filing are the company's effort to add suppliers and contract manufacturers, its U.S. manufacturing expansion, and the "channel pricing programs" it uses to clear inventory. The filing also notes that inventory provisions and channel price cuts reduce margins, so these tools are costly. A useful test is whether NVIDIA can keep lead times and commitments in line with orders without further provisions.

## Risk 2: Export controls and loss of access to China

**Industry vertical risk.** This is the risk most specific to NVIDIA among the three. Its products are subject to licensing rules that the filing says change "at any time," and the company says it "may be unable to sell our inventory of such products and may be unable to develop replacement products not subject to the licensing requirements." Unlike supply-chain or competition risk, which also exist for peers, this risk arises from government decisions that can remove a product from a market in a single action. The filing also says that the Chinese government has "encouraged customers to purchase from our China-based competitors and discouraged customers from purchasing, importing, or using our data center products." The competitive effect is therefore two-sided: U.S. rules restrict NVIDIA's sales, and Chinese policy steers customers away from it.

**Cyclicality.** This is not a cyclical risk in the usual sense. It is a policy-driven risk that has moved in steps. The filing lists a sequence of restrictions: August 2022, July 2023, October 2023, April 2025 (H20), and the February 2026 H200 license. Each step has had its own financial effect, and the filing states that "over the past three years, we have been subject to a series of shifting and expanding export control restrictions." The pattern is one of tightening and partial relief rather than a cycle with a predictable trough.

**Risk quantification.** The filing gives the most concrete numbers of any risk:

- A $4.5 billion charge in the first quarter of fiscal 2026 "associated with H20 for excess inventory and purchase obligations, as the demand for H20 products diminished."
- Approximately $60 million of H20 revenue generated under licenses granted in August 2025, against that charge.
- An expectation from USG officials that the government would receive "15% or more of the revenue generated from licensed sales," which the USG did not codify in a regulation.
- For the H200 license granted in February 2026, "to date, we have not generated any revenue," and any H200 shipped "will be subject to a 25% tariff upon importation into the United States."
- The statement that "as of the end of fiscal year 2026, we were effectively foreclosed from competing in China's data center computing/compute market."
- On September 15, 2025, China's antitrust regulators published a preliminary finding that NVIDIA's compliance with U.S. export controls "discriminated unfairly against customers in the China market and therefore violated the terms of China's approval of our Mellanox acquisition." The filing lists possible consequences including financial penalties and restrictions on networking products.

The $60 million in licensed H20 revenue set against a $4.5 billion charge gives a concrete sense of how little licensed sales offset the write-down. The filing does not give China revenue for any period, so I cannot state China's share of total revenue.

**Downside protections.** The filing describes few protections. The company says it "seek[s] to strictly comply with all applicable export control regulators" and that its Gaming and other products outside China are also exposed. The filing notes that export controls may affect the "Gaming" supply chain because products "are warehoused in and distributed from Hong Kong." Diversification across geographies offers some relief: the filing says 31% of fiscal 2026 revenue came from sales outside the United States. That also means the remaining share, which is arithmetically about 69%, is not directly exposed to foreign sales limits, although it can still be affected by the broader rules. Diversification across the Data Center, Gaming, Professional Visualization, and Automotive segments limits the damage from any one market, but the filing's own description of Data Center as the dominant concern is not quantified in the provided data.

## Risk 3: Customer concentration and counterparty exposure

**Industry vertical risk.** The filing provides a clear concentration profile: "sales to one direct customer represented 22% of total revenue and sales to another direct customer represented 14% of total revenue," both primarily in the Compute & Networking segment. Taken together, those two customers account for 36% of total revenue (an arithmetic sum of the filing's figures). The filing also says that "we estimate some [indirect customers] individually representing 10% or more of our revenue." This is more concentrated than a typical diversified hardware supplier, but the filing does not give peer comparisons, so I cannot say how far outside the norm it is.

The risk is compounded by how sales are contracted. The filing states that "most of our sales are made on a purchase order basis, our customers can generally cancel, change, or delay product purchase commitments with little notice to us and without penalty." Customers with this flexibility, and with in-house alternatives, have bargaining power over NVIDIA.

**Cyclicality.** The filing links the counterparty risk to economic stress: "reduced profitability of customers, which may cause them to scale back operations, exit businesses, file for bankruptcy protection," and "increased credit and collectability risks." It also says the company "has granted and may continue to grant extended payment terms to some customers, particularly during macroeconomic downturns." Cyclical pressure therefore reaches NVIDIA through its receivables as well as its orders.

**Risk quantification.** The main quantified points are:

- The 22% and 14% direct customer shares for fiscal 2026.
- Extended payment terms and "customer bad debt write-offs" listed as factors affecting results.
- The filing states the company "have been asked to offer financing arrangements to support our customers' and partners' buildout of datacenter infrastructure" and "have not entered into any financing arrangements." It also says that "financing arrangements, if undertaken, may in some circumstances result in lower upfront cash flows associated with extended payment terms or payment terms made over a multi-year term."
- The company has "entered and may in the future enter into commercial arrangements, including long-term capacity purchase obligations and financial guarantees."
- The filing states it is "finalizing an investment and partnership agreement with OpenAI" and that "there is no assurance that we will enter into" the agreement or that the transaction will be completed.

The filing does not quantify receivables, guarantees, or the size of any financing commitment, so the exposure cannot be sized from the provided data.

**Downside protections.** The filing describes a weaker protective position than the other two risks suggest. Sales are largely on purchase orders without penalty for cancellation, and the filing does not describe long-term customer volume contracts. The company's main cushions are its liquid investment portfolio, which the filing says is "a majority" invested in "USG securities," and its use of credit insurance for some customers with extended terms, which the filing lists as a factor. The filing notes that "our ability to obtain credit insurance for customers with extended payment terms" is itself a variable. Insurance is also described as limited: the filing says "our insurance providers may be unable or unwilling to pay a claim" and that some policies "have large deductibles and broad exclusions."

## Overall assessment

The filing supports a view that NVIDIA's largest near-term financial risks are in its own commitments and in government decisions, not in its end markets. The supply-demand risk has already produced write-downs, and the filing says the exposure grows as prepaid and purchase obligations rise. The export-control risk has already produced a $4.5 billion charge and an effective exit from China's data center market, and the filing states that the company's "competitive position and future results will be further harmed, over the long term, if the restrictions remain in place or are expanded." The customer concentration risk has not yet produced a stated loss, but it sits alongside a sales model that lets customers cancel with little notice and a growing set of financing and capacity commitments.

A balanced reading also has to note what the filing does not show. It does not give total revenue, segment profit, the size of purchase obligations, or China revenue for any period. Without those, the magnitude of each risk relative to earnings cannot be determined from these resources. The filing also presents some favorable developments without quantifying them: the H20 licenses generated about $60 million, and the H200 license has produced no revenue so far.

The view in this analysis would be wrong if any of the following occurred. First, if the H200 program produces meaningful China revenue after the 25% tariff, the export-control assessment would need revision. Second, if lead times fall and inventory provisions stop recurring, the supply risk would be smaller than this analysis suggests. Third, if the two largest direct customers fall well below their fiscal 2026 shares, the concentration risk would ease. Fourth, if China's antitrust preliminary finding does not lead to penalties or restrictions on the networking business, the Mellanox-related exposure would be lower. Finally, if the OpenAI agreement is completed on terms that the filing describes without the counterparty exposure it lists, that would reduce concern about the financing side of the third risk, although the filing does not describe those terms.

*Note on method: the calculations in this analysis are limited to arithmetic on figures stated in the filing (for example, the 36% combined share of the two largest direct customers and the 69% non-foreign share implied by the 31% figure). No external industry or peer data were used.*
