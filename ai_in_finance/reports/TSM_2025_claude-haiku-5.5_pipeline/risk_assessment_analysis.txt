# Top Three Risks for TSMC: Customer Concentration, Trade and Export-Control Exposure, and Physical Operating Disruption

The risk factors point to three exposures that matter most. The first is customer concentration in a cyclical end market. The ten largest customers accounted for 70%, 76% and 78% of net revenue in 2023, 2024 and 2025, and the largest single customer's share was 25%, 22% and 19% in the same years. The second is trade and export-control exposure, because the majority of principal production and revenue sits in the R.O.C. while the company sells globally into a regulatory environment that changed several times in the past two years. The third is physical operating disruption, including earthquakes, power shortages and sole-sourced inputs. Each is described below against the four requested aspects. The filing does not benchmark these risks against other industries, so the industry-vertical comparison is limited to what the filing itself says.

## Risk 1: Customer Concentration and Cyclical End Markets

**Industry vertical risk.** The filing describes the electronics and semiconductor industries as cyclical, with "significant and occasionally prolonged periods of downturn and overcapacity." It does not compare that cyclicality with other sectors, so no ranking is possible from this document. Within the company's own disclosure, the risk is elevated by a structural shift: the filing attributes customer concentration partly to "the structural shift to HPC and smartphone applications," and notes that "only a limited number of customers" are successfully exploiting the new business model. Its most common competitive threat is customers who design their own chips and work directly with foundries, which the filing says "could lead to significant variations in our sales if the growth of their products and services, particularly in the AI sector, is volatile or not sustainable."

**Cyclicality.** The filing states that the company is "dependent on the demand of electronics and semiconductor companies," so a downturn in those industries flows directly into foundry demand. The cost structure amplifies the cycle. The company says that "because we own most of our manufacturing capacities, a significant portion of our operating costs is fixed" and that these costs "do not decline when customer demand or our capacity utilization rates drop." The filing also notes that margins improve when utilization rises because fixed costs are spread over more output. The result is that revenue declines and margin declines can be larger than the revenue decline alone. Some lines or tools may be suspended during downturns, but the filing warns that restoring capacity quickly on a sudden upturn may not be possible. The filing does not quantify the cyclical sensitivity of margins, so the size of this effect cannot be stated from the document.

**Risk quantification.** The concentration figures are the main quantitative evidence. The largest two customers accounted for 36% of revenue in 2023 (25% and 11%), 34% in 2024 (22% and 12%) and 36% in 2025 (19% and 17%). This is illustrative arithmetic from the disclosed shares, and it shows that the two-customer total held roughly steady while the mix shifted toward the second customer, whose share rose from 12% to 17% in 2025. The ten-customer share rose by eight percentage points over two years (70% to 78%), so the increase came from customers beyond the top two. The filing also cites customer consolidation through mergers and acquisitions, which "will further decrease the overall number of our customer pool," and export controls on major customers that could reduce their demand. The loss or curtailment of one of the top customers is named in the filing as a direct cause of adverse results. What would prove this risk overstated is a decline in the top-ten share over the next filings, combined with new customers in HPC or automotive growing enough to offset the concentration.

**Downside protections.** The filing offers limited protection against this risk. It states that revenue comes from "hundreds of customers worldwide," which spreads exposure beyond the top ten. It mentions no long-term contract terms, backlog figures or pricing commitments, so those protections cannot be assessed from the document. The filing's own discussion of fixed costs describes the opposite of a protection: the same fixed-cost base that raises operating leverage on the upside raises downside exposure when demand falls.

## Risk 2: Trade, Tariff and Export-Control Exposure

**Industry vertical risk.** The filing presents trade and sanctions risk as sector-specific for semiconductors, noting that tariffs, export controls and sanctions "have been particularly impactful to the semiconductor industry and related markets." The company's own risk profile is made more complex by its location. The filing states that "the majority of our principal executive officers and our principal production facilities are located in the R.O.C., and the majority of our net revenue is derived from our operations in the R.O.C." It also says that a "significant percentage" of revenue comes from outside the R.O.C., and that the U.S. is a market from which the company derives "a substantial portion of our revenue." Cross-border policy changes therefore reach both the cost base and the sales base.

**Cyclicality.** This risk is less cyclical than customer demand but more episodic. It is driven by policy events. The filing lists a sequence of them: the April 2025 10% baseline tariff, the reciprocal tariffs collected from August 2025, the February 2026 Supreme Court ruling that declared the IEEPA-based tariffs unlawful, a replacement tariff under Section 122, two Section 301 investigations opened in March 2026, and a Section 232 proclamation in December 2025 imposing a 25% tariff on certain advanced computing chips unless imported for designated use in the U.S. The January 2026 trade agreement with Taiwan is described as reducing reciprocal tariffs on Taiwanese goods to no more than 15%. The filing says the effects of these changes "are still unclear," which means the cost and demand impact has not yet settled.

**Risk quantification.** The filing gives several concrete figures. Every 1% depreciation of the U.S. dollar against the NT dollar would reduce operating margin by approximately 0.3 percentage points, based on 2025 results. Substantially all sales are in U.S. dollars, and over half of capital expenditures are in other currencies, mainly U.S. dollars, euros and Japanese yen. The Nanjing fab previously operated under a Validated End-User authorization that expired in December 2025; it now operates under an annual export license that the filing says "there is no assurance" will not be terminated or renewed. The filing discloses an October 2024 notice to U.S. and Taiwan authorities that a customer's chip made by the company "might have been diverted to a restricted entity," with continuing cooperation on information requests. The filing also says the company's current results "have not been materially affected" by expanded export controls. That statement is useful, but it describes the past, and the filing makes clear the exposure is forward-looking. A license non-renewal at Nanjing, an adverse export-control finding, or a tariff on a broader category of Semiconductor Items would each be a clear test of this risk.

**Downside protections.** The filing identifies several partial mitigants. The reciprocal tariffs excluded semiconductors and semiconductor equipment, and the trade agreement reportedly gives preferential Section 232 treatment to Taiwanese producers investing in U.S. capacity. The company is building capacity in Arizona, Kumamoto and Dresden, and has received or agreed to government incentives, including up to US$6.6 billion in direct funding and up to US$5 billion in proposed loans for TSMC Arizona under the U.S. CHIPS Act, and up to EUR5 billion of state aid for ESMC in Germany. These protections carry conditions. The filing states that incentives "may be subject to conditions and requirements," including restrictions on expansion in "foreign countries of concern," and that noncompliance could lead to forfeiture or repayment. The incentives therefore reduce some cost exposure but add conditions that constrain where the company can expand.

## Risk 3: Physical Operating Disruption

**Industry vertical risk.** The filing describes the company's operations as "vulnerable to interruptions from various natural and man-made disasters," and notes that most production facilities, as well as those of many suppliers and customers, are in areas susceptible to natural disasters. The filing's reliance on a single geography for most of its fabs is the main point of differentiation. It does not compare this with other industries. Within semiconductors, the filing notes that the company is dependent on a limited number of equipment suppliers and that some raw materials come from sole-sourced suppliers.

**Cyclicality.** Physical disruption is not cyclical in the economic sense. It occurs at random, but the filing states that the consequences can be prolonged. A "prolonged disruption to our operations, or those of our suppliers, could significantly reduce our manufacturing capacity and lead to the loss of important customers." The filing also says power problems may become more frequent in some conditions: "government measures taken in response to severe weather events, such as water rationing... may materially affect our operations." Utility price increases are described as a recurring pattern, and the filing says the company "has experienced, and may continue to encounter utility price increases."

**Risk quantification.** The filing gives two earthquake loss figures: approximately NT$3 billion in the second quarter of 2024 and NT$5.3 billion in the first quarter of 2025, both net of insurance claims. Illustratively, the two events together total about NT$8.3 billion net of insurance. The filing does not state gross losses, the insurance recovery amounts, or any effect on capacity or customer deliveries, so the financial impact beyond these two charges cannot be estimated. The filing also cites sole-sourced raw materials and a limited supplier base for equipment as sources of supply risk, but it provides no share of purchases or number of suppliers. The risk would be considered more severe if the next disclosure showed larger earthquake losses, repeated outages affecting output, or a sole-sourced input shortage that delayed deliveries.

**Downside protections.** The filing describes some measures: insurance claims offset the earthquake losses, the company has "various collaborative business models and risk management contingencies with suppliers," and it can "temporarily reassign thousands of our engineers" between sites. Those reassignment capabilities help the company recover from localized problems, but the filing lists them as a benefit that depends on the regional employment rules and travel restrictions that could limit them. The filing does not describe redundancy in fab capacity or inventory levels, so the scale of protection against a multi-month shutdown cannot be assessed.

## Overall Assessment

The three risks are connected. Customer concentration makes the company's revenue sensitive to a small number of buyers, and those buyers are affected by the same trade and export-control measures that the company faces directly. A large customer that loses access to a market because of a sanctions regime or tariff reduces TSMC's volume while the fixed-cost base stays in place. The physical risk compounds this because a disruption at a single production location in a high-exposure region reduces output for the same customers who already represent most revenue. The filing's most important feature is that its mitigants are mostly qualitative. It says the company diversifies across hundreds of customers and has ecosystem flexibility, but it does not disclose the revenue share of the broader customer base, the contract structure with top customers, or the share of capacity outside the R.O.C. that would be needed to offset a regional disruption. The government incentive programs reduce some capital and cost exposure, but they come with conditions tied to where and with whom the company can operate.

Several developments would show this assessment is too pessimistic. The top-ten customer share would need to decline in future filings while total revenue grows. The Nanjing annual license would need to be renewed without restriction. The U.S. tariff framework would need to settle on rates that leave semiconductors and equipment excluded in practice, not just in the initial proclamations. Earthquake losses would need to stay small and insurance recoveries would need to cover most of the gross amount. Each of these is a specific, observable test in future filings. Conversely, a decline in the top-ten share, a loss or non-renewal of the Nanjing license, a new export-control action affecting a major customer, or another large net earthquake charge would each indicate that the risks described here are becoming more material to results.
