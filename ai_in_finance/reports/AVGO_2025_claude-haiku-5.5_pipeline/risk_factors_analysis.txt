# Broadcom Inc.: Top Three Risks from the FY2025 Risk Factors

The three risks that matter most in this filing are: (1) concentrated, AI-driven demand from a small set of customers, some of whom may need deferred or financed payment terms; (2) dependence on one wafer foundry and a small group of materials suppliers; and (3) gross margin pressure, which the filing ties partly to the shift toward selling or leasing AI racks and systems based on Broadcom's XPUs. I ranked them by how directly they touch revenue, cash flow and margin, and by how often the filing returns to them. Tax exposure, debt and regulation are real but are secondary here, and I return to them at the end.

The resources contain no peer-industry data, no segment revenue, no dollar revenue or gross margin figures, and no cash flow statement. The comparisons below are therefore qualitative and rest on the filing's own language. Where I say a number is illustrative, it is a rough calculation of mine, not a figure from the filing.

## Risk 1: AI-Driven Customer Concentration and Demand Cyclicality

**Industry vertical risk.** The filing describes the semiconductor industry as "highly cyclical," subject to "rapid price erosion, wide fluctuations in product supply and demand, constant and rapid technological change." It also states that the industry "has experienced a significant upturn, which may not be sustainable." The filing does not compare this vertical with other industries, so I cannot rank it against them with data. The language does place it at the high end of cyclicality in the filing's own terms, and the risk is sharpened by the fact that Broadcom's software business, which the filing describes as relying on multi-year enterprise license agreements, is a different and in some respects steadier revenue stream than its semiconductor business.

**Cyclicality.** The filing ties demand to customer capital spending. It says customers may "reduce the amount of products or decline to purchase from us due to reduced capital expenditure spending, lack of access to sufficient capital, downturn in their business, purchases from our competitors or their internal development of the products." It also says some AI customers "may have constrained resources or capital and may be unable to pay for their required AI infrastructure and/or seek alternative financings or novel or deferred payment models." That is the mechanism by which an AI spending slowdown would reach Broadcom: first through order reductions, then through receivables and credit exposure. The filing also notes that the timing and extent of "delivery of and payment for" AI racks or systems is listed among the factors that cause quarterly fluctuations, so the cycle shows up in quarterly results as well as annual ones.

**Risk quantification.** The filing gives these figures for fiscal year 2025:

- Sales to distributors were 48% of net revenue.
- Aggregate sales through all channels to the top five end customers were approximately 40% of net revenue. This is management's belief, not a reported segment figure.
- Total indebtedness as of November 2, 2025 was $67,120 million. This matters here because the filing says customer financing arrangements "may increase our exposure to credit or customer default risks," so Broadcom may end up carrying customer credit risk on its own balance sheet while also servicing its own debt.

The filing also says the top customers "may make and have made greater demands on us with regards to pricing and contractual terms," including requests to lease rather than purchase AI racks. A 40% top-five concentration means that the loss or reduction of a single large customer could move revenue materially, though the filing does not quantify that sensitivity. An illustrative check: if one top-five customer accounted for one-eighth of that 40% (about 5% of revenue), a 50% cut in its orders would reduce total revenue by roughly 2.5%, before any offsetting volume from other customers. The filing provides no basis for the one-eighth split, so this is only a sizing exercise.

**Downside protections.** The filing offers limited contractual protection against this risk. The purchase arrangements it describes often include "pricing schedules or methodologies that apply regardless of the volume of products purchased," which can protect pricing but do not guarantee volume. Software license agreements are a partial offset: the filing states that some software agreements "under which customers do not have the right to terminate" produce more predictable revenue. The filing also notes that its customer contracts "typically contain warranty and indemnification provisions," which are protections for customers rather than for Broadcom. The filing does not describe any reserve or credit insurance program that would cover losses from AI customer defaults. Its statement that "we may not generate the amount of revenue or free cash flow... that we or investors expect" is the most direct admission that these protections are not sufficient to rule out a shortfall.

**What would prove this view wrong.** Broadcom's top-customer orders would have to hold steady through a period of tighter AI infrastructure financing, and the company would have to avoid the deferred-payment and leasing structures the filing describes. Evidence that AI customers are paying on schedule without extended terms would weaken this concern.

## Risk 2: Dependence on One Foundry and a Few Materials Suppliers

**Industry vertical risk.** Broadcom runs what it calls a "primarily outsourced manufacturing business model." That structure is common in semiconductors, but the filing makes the concentration unusually specific. The filing does not compare this with other companies, so I cannot say whether the concentration is high relative to peers. The filing does say that qualifying a new foundry or supplier "is a lengthy and often expensive process" and that "there is often no readily available alternative source," which makes switching costly regardless of how peers compare.

**Cyclicality.** Supply constraints tend to tighten in upcycles. The filing says its foundries "have had capacity constraints in times of unprecedented demand" and that suppliers "have previously, and may in the future, extend lead times, limit supplies, place products on allocation or increase prices." That means the risk is not only that demand falls but that supply becomes unavailable precisely when demand is strongest, and the filing states that TSMC "has raised, and may in the future raise, their prices to manufacture our wafers."

**Risk quantification.** The filing provides these data points:

- Approximately 95% of wafers manufactured by Broadcom's contract manufacturers in fiscal year 2025 were produced by TSMC.
- During fiscal year 2025, approximately two-thirds of manufacturing materials were purchased from five suppliers, some of which are single source.
- Substantially all manufacturing services are on a purchase order basis with no minimum quantities, and there are no long-term capacity commitments.
- Two of Broadcom's own facilities are sole sources: Fort Collins, Colorado for FBAR filters used in many wireless devices, and Breinigsville, Pennsylvania for InP-based wafers used in fibre optics products.

The filing says TSMC "could choose or be required to materially prioritize capacity for other customers or reduce or eliminate deliveries to us on short notice," and that TSMC also makes wafers for some of Broadcom's competitors. The 95% figure therefore means that a disruption at a single company would affect nearly all of Broadcom's foundry output.

**Downside protections.** The filing describes some mitigating factors but few hard protections. Broadcom states that it "continuously manage[s] product availability and costs in our supply chain." It notes that its own fabrication facilities, Singapore among them, give it some internal capacity. Its customer and supplier arrangements carry indemnification provisions, but the filing says that for CMs and suppliers "may or may not be required to indemnify us." The company also has self-insured retentions for reasonably estimable liabilities and says that, to date, "such events have not had a material adverse effect on our business." The filing does not describe a multi-foundry qualification program that would shorten a switch, so the protection rests largely on the company's operational response rather than on contract terms.

**What would prove this view wrong.** Evidence that Broadcom has qualified meaningful volume at a second foundry for its leading products, or that TSMC allocation to Broadcom has remained stable and its pricing increases have been absorbed without margin loss, would reduce this concern.

## Risk 3: Gross Margin Pressure from Pricing and the Shift to AI Racks

**Industry vertical risk.** The filing identifies price erosion and competitive pricing as persistent features of the vertical. It says competition is characterized by "often aggressive pricing practices," and that competitors who are larger, consolidating, or backed by home-country governments may pressure margins. The filing does not compare Broadcom's margin volatility with other companies, so the relative ranking remains qualitative.

**Cyclicality.** The filing ties gross margin to "level of capacity utilization," "industry oversupply," and "lower demand." Fixed costs magnify this effect. The filing states that "a significant amount of our operating expenses are relatively fixed in nature" and that a failure to adjust spending quickly enough "could magnify the adverse impact of such revenue shortfall." In a downturn, the combination of falling prices and fixed costs would push margin down faster than revenue.

**Risk quantification.** The resources contain no gross margin percentage, so I cannot put a number on the decline or on its probable size. The filing does provide these qualitative and structural facts:

- The gross margin for semiconductor solutions "has typically been lower than our infrastructure software solutions." Mix therefore matters: a shift toward semiconductors lowers gross margin.
- The filing states directly that "the sale or leasing of AI racks or systems based on our XPUs will likely increase our operating margin but compress or lower future gross margin, which would adversely impact our stock price."

That second statement is the most important disclosure in this section. It means that management expects the new business model to improve operating margin while reducing gross margin, and it names the stock price consequence. The filing also says that making alternative financings or "novel or deferred payment models" available for AI racks "could have a material adverse effect on our revenue, free cash flow and gross margin."

**Downside protections.** The filing offers software revenue, which carries higher gross margin than semiconductors, as a partial buffer, along with the contractual features noted above. The filing does not describe a pricing floor or any price-protection mechanism for semiconductor products. Its only stated mitigating action is to "seek to evolve our business strategy" and to take on new business models, which is itself a source of the margin pressure rather than protection against it.

**What would prove this view wrong.** Reported gross margin holding at or above prior levels as AI rack sales grow, and an operating margin improvement that does not come with a sustained drop in gross margin, would show that the compression the filing anticipates is smaller than feared. Conversely, continued increases in customer financing or leasing terms without a matching change in pricing would confirm it.

## Secondary Risks and Overall Assessment

The three risks above are linked. The same AI customers who drive demand are the ones seeking deferred payment, lease structures and aggressive pricing. The same concentration that gives Broadcom scale with TSMC makes a foundry disruption more damaging. And the same business model shift that the company expects to lift operating margin is the one the filing says will compress gross margin. A stress in AI spending would therefore hit revenue, credit exposure, and margin at once, which is more severe than any one of these risks in isolation.

Three other risks deserve weight, though the filing gives them less space. First, tax: the filing says many countries have implemented or are implementing a global minimum tax, which Broadcom "expect[s] to materially increase our effective tax rate and cash tax costs for our fiscal year ending November 1, 2026." It also discloses that its tax incentives decreased the provision for income taxes by approximately $2,709 million in fiscal year 2025, and increased diluted net income per share by $0.56. The $2,709 million is stated before U.S. foreign tax credits, so the filing does not allow a precise per-share sensitivity to a loss of those incentives. Second, debt: aggregate indebtedness was $67,120 million at November 2, 2025, and the filing notes that a credit rating downgrade could "materially adversely affect our business." Third, the VMware spin-off from Dell in November 2021 carries a contingent tax exposure if the transaction is not tax-free under Section 355, and the filing says Broadcom may be required to indemnify Dell for resulting liabilities. The filing does not quantify that exposure.

Taken together, the filing presents a company with substantial downside exposure concentrated in AI demand, supply and margin, and with limited contractual protection against a sharp downturn in any of them. The filing is candid about this. It states that the AI-related growth "may not be sustainable," that gross margin will likely compress, and that quarterly comparisons "may not be meaningful or reliable indicators of our future performance." An investor relying on this filing would need to monitor AI customer payment terms, the TSMC allocation and pricing, and gross margin as the business mix moves toward AI racks. Those three indicators would show most directly whether the risks described here are materializing.
